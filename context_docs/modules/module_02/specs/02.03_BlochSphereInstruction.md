## Open questions for review

Each has a recommendation. Decisions here change the draft below.

1. **How to describe a state beside the sphere at Beginner.** 02.02's Beginner view used real amplitudes only, but a point off the x–z circle needs a phase. *Recommendation:* at Beginner show the two **magnitudes** (cos and sin of half the polar angle, shown as decimals) and the **phase as an angle in degrees**, with no complex notation. At Intermediate show the complex amplitudes from `QedQubit.format(QedQubit.fromBloch(θ, φ))`. This also delivers on 02.02's "some states need more than real numbers".
2. **Manipulating the sphere.** The taxonomy asks for a "manipulable sphere". Dragging a projected point is ambiguous (front or back hemisphere) and hard to make keyboard-accessible. *Recommendation:* no canvas dragging in V1. The primary controls are two sliders (polar angle and phase angle, each with a typed value) and preset buttons that snap to named states. The canvas is a display that moves with them. *Question:* is drag a hard requirement?
3. **Naming the two other equator states.** The taxonomy says "two further equator states". *Recommendation:* name them |+i⟩ and |−i⟩ at Beginner, as "named for the direction they point", with phase angles 90° and 270°, and add them to a six-row table of named points. They are not in `QedQubit.PRESETS` yet (four presets). *Question:* add `plusI` and `minusI` to `QedQubit.PRESETS`, which changes what 02.02's generated tables show, or keep a separate list local to this page? I recommend a separate list in a small exported constant in `QedQubit` (for example `BLOCH_POINTS`), so 02.02's tables stay as they are.
4. **Showing "height decides the probabilities".** *Recommendation:* draw a dashed line from the point straight to the vertical axis, with a marker on the axis, and label the pole ends with their outcome ("certain 0" and "certain 1") and the equator with "50/50". Keep the probability bars beside the sphere, synced. At Beginner give no formula; at Intermediate show the vector (x, y, z) and P(0) = (1 + z)/2.
5. **Comparing two states.** *Recommendation:* a "Pin this state" button that leaves a ghost marker, so the learner can move to |−⟩ and see it opposite |+⟩ at the same height. This supports "same counts, different states" from 02.01 and 02.02.
6. **Extending `QedBloch.render`.** Needed: markers with labels, axes, the projection, a depth cue, a second (pinned) vector. *Recommendation:* add an options argument that defaults to today's drawing, so Module 01 is unaffected. Settle the meaning of `project`'s `depth` sign when adding the depth cue. `render` is not unit-testable, so it is verified in the browser.
7. **How far the Intermediate layer goes.** The taxonomy lists the formula, how (θ, φ) relate to amplitudes, global-phase-equivalent descriptions as one point, and the limits for multi-qubit systems and mixed states. *Recommendation:* build it as one gated block with a level-aware control (an overall-phase slider that changes the displayed amplitudes but not the point), and **name** the limits in words (two qubits do not fit on one sphere; mixed states are not on the surface) without teaching them. Advanced names mixed states inside the ball and the density-matrix view only.
8. **Glossary.** Existing entries to reuse: Bloch Sphere, Pure State, Mixed State, Density Matrix, Rotation Gate, Relative Phase, Global Phase, Quantum State, Basis State, Computational Basis. *Recommendation:* add one new entry, *Orthogonal States* (antipodal points on the sphere; inner product zero), introduced at Intermediate only, and decide in the terminology check whether the |+i⟩/|−i⟩ names need entries (I would not add them).
9. **Module 01 overlap.** Module 01's page already draws a Bloch sphere as a state readout. *Recommendation:* the 02.03 page opens by naming that readout and says this page is where the sphere becomes a tool: what the poles, equator, height, and angle mean, and what the sphere cannot show. Do not repeat Module 01's θ and φ sliders as a bare demo.
10. **A teaser for gates.** The taxonomy asks that phase "can matter after a further gate". *Recommendation:* one sentence only, with no gate named or shown: "Later pages show an operation that turns a phase difference into a difference you can see." Nothing is taught.

## Goal

Teach the learner to picture the state of one qubit as a point on a sphere: how to read the point, what its height tells you, what its angle around the equator tells you, and what the picture cannot show.

Central progression:

`A STATE → A POINT ON A SPHERE → HEIGHT DECIDES THE PROBABILITIES → THE ANGLE AROUND THE EQUATOR IS PHASE → THE PICTURE HAS LIMITS`

Key conceptual transition: **"A state can be pictured as a point on a sphere. How high the point is decides the probabilities. How far around it is carries the phase."**

Builds on 02.02's list of amplitudes, whose signs were easy to miss. Ends by asking how a state can be moved from one point to another (02.04).

## Learning outcomes

**Beginner (the Core Learning Contract).** The learner should be able to:

1. Explain that the Bloch sphere is a picture of the state of one qubit, not a physical object the qubit sits in.
2. Find |0⟩ and |1⟩ at the poles, and |+⟩, |−⟩, |+i⟩, and |−i⟩ on the equator.
3. Read the outcome probabilities from a point's height: certain 0 at the north pole, 50/50 on the equator, certain 1 at the south pole.
4. Explain that moving around the equator changes the state but not the Z-basis probabilities.
5. Identify |+⟩ and |−⟩ as opposite points on the equator with the same height.
6. Describe phase as an angle around the equator.
7. Explain that a point on the sphere is a state, never a measurement result.
8. State that the sphere pictures a pure state of one qubit, up to a shared overall phase.
9. Ask how a state can be moved from one point to another.

**Intermediate (gated).** Write |ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + e^{iφ} sin(θ/2)|1⟩ and relate (θ, φ) to the amplitudes; recognize that descriptions differing by a global phase are the same point; name the sphere's limits for several qubits and for mixed states.

**Advanced (gated).** Name mixed states inside the ball and the density-matrix view.

## Experience-level scope

**Beginner (V1) teaches:** the sphere as a map; poles and equator; the six named states; height and probabilities; phase as an angle; the |+⟩ versus |−⟩ comparison; a single sentence on pure states and the shared overall phase; point ≠ measurement result; a one-sentence, nameless teaser that phase can matter after a further operation.

**Intermediate (gated now):** the formula and its relation to the amplitudes; the Bloch vector (x, y, z) and P(0) = (1 + z)/2; global-phase-equivalent descriptions as one point, with a level-aware overall-phase control; the limits for several qubits and for mixed states, named; opposite points as orthogonal states.

**Advanced (named only):** mixed states inside the ball; the density-matrix view.

**Not on this page (any level):** gates, rotations as operations, how to move a state (02.04), tensor products (02.05), the Born rule as a formula, physical rotations, noise, and decoherence (Module 04).

## Page structure (draft)

1. **From a List of Numbers to a Picture.** Reconnect to 02.02: a list of numbers hides the sign and is hard to compare. Ask: "How can we picture a state, including its sign, instead of reading a list of numbers?" Name Module 01's sphere readout and say this page makes the sphere a tool.

2. **A Map of States.** Introduce the sphere as a globe-like picture of one qubit's states. Poles: |0⟩ (north, certain 0) and |1⟩ (south, certain 1). Equator: |+⟩, |−⟩, |+i⟩, |−i⟩. A six-row table of the named points with their polar and phase angles. Say clearly that the sphere is a map of states, not a place where the qubit physically sits.

3. **Main Interactive: "Sphere Explorer."** Two sliders and presets move a point on the sphere, with the amplitude description and the probability bars synced. See below.

4. **Height Decides the Probabilities.** Height is what sets the outcome probabilities: the north pole is certain 0, the equator is 50/50, the south pole is certain 1, and in between it slides smoothly. Show the dashed projection to the vertical axis. Checks (see below).

5. **Around the Equator: Phase as an Angle.** Moving around the equator keeps the height, so the probabilities do not change, but the state does. Compare |+⟩ and |−⟩ with a pinned ghost: same height, opposite sides. Connect to 02.02: the real-amplitude states the learner built sit on one circle, where the phase is 0° or 180°. One sentence: later pages show an operation that turns a phase difference into a difference you can see.

6. **Beyond the Core.** Gated block (heading inside the Intermediate block, as on 02.01 and 02.02).
   - `[Intermediate]` The formula and its relation to the amplitudes; the Bloch vector and P(0) = (1 + z)/2; global-phase-equivalent descriptions as one point, with the overall-phase control; opposite points are orthogonal states; the limits for several qubits and for mixed states, named.
   - `[Advanced]` A very short block naming mixed states inside the ball and the density-matrix view.

7. **From Picture to Motion.** Bridge. A point can sit anywhere on the sphere, but we do not yet know how to get it from one place to another. Ask: "How can we move a state from one point to another?" Next: **Quantum Gates →**

## Main Interactive: "Sphere Explorer"

- **Controls:** a polar-angle slider (0° to 180°) and a phase-angle slider (0° to 360°), each with a typed value; preset buttons for |0⟩, |1⟩, |+⟩, |−⟩, |+i⟩, and |−i⟩; "Pin this state"; reset.
- **Display, kept in separate places:**
  - the sphere, with labelled named points, the axes, the dashed projection onto the vertical axis, and the pinned ghost;
  - the **state description**: at Beginner, the two magnitudes (decimals) and the phase angle in degrees; at Intermediate, the complex-amplitude written form;
  - the **probability bars** for outcome 0 and outcome 1, with percentages.
- **Reading the sphere in words:** a one-line caption such as "Height: halfway, so 50/50. Phase: 90°."
- **Pinning:** the pinned state stays as a ghost marker with its own label; the learner can move the live point and compare. Unpin or reset clears it.
- **Intermediate behavior (level-aware, gated):** add an overall-phase slider that changes the displayed amplitudes but not the point on the sphere, show (x, y, z), and show P(0) = (1 + z)/2. The Beginner behavior never depends on the level.
- **Accessibility:** the sliders and presets are the non-3D alternative and work without the canvas. The canvas has `role="img"` and an `aria-label` that updates with the state (`QedBloch.formatText`). A text readout and a table of the named points give the same information. Everything is keyboard-operable, and an aria-live region summarizes the state.
- **Scope fence:** no gates, no rotations as operations, no drag on the canvas, no measurement or shots.

### Checks (taxonomy section 7)

- *Prediction task:* "A state sits on the equator. Predict the probability of outcome 0" (50%), then compare with the bars.
- *Conceptual question:* which of several pairs of points give the same Z-basis probabilities? (Any two points at the same height, for example |+⟩ and |−⟩; |0⟩ and |1⟩ do not.) Feedback explains each choice.
- *Transfer task:* make outcome 1 happen 25% of the time (polar angle 60°, any phase). The feedback notes that the phase does not matter.

## Important pedagogical constraints (draft)

Do not imply:
- `A POINT ON THE SPHERE = A MEASUREMENT RESULT`. The point is the state; a measurement returns 0 or 1.
- `THE SPHERE = PHYSICAL SPACE`. The qubit is not an arrow pointing somewhere in the room, and nothing here is a physical rotation.
- `NORTH POLE = UP` in any physical sense. It is the state |0⟩.
- `PHASE CHANGES THE PROBABILITIES` (in the Z basis). Moving around the equator keeps the height.
- That |+⟩ and |−⟩ can be told apart by Z-basis counts, or that this page shows how to tell them apart.
- That every state of every system fits on this sphere. Say plainly that it pictures one qubit in a pure state, up to a shared overall phase.
- That the sphere's axes are the three coordinate directions of the lab.

Do not teach here: gates, how to move a point, tensor products, entanglement, the Born rule as a formula, physical rotations, noise, or decoherence.

## End state (draft)

The learner leaves with: `A STATE → A POINT ON A SPHERE → HEIGHT SETS THE PROBABILITIES → THE ANGLE AROUND THE EQUATOR IS PHASE → THE PICTURE HAS LIMITS`.

Takeaway: *A qubit's state can be pictured as a point on a sphere. Height sets the probabilities and the angle around the equator carries the phase, and the picture shows one qubit in a pure state only.*
