________________________________________
Goal
Teach the learner how to picture the state of one qubit as a point on a sphere, building on 02.02's list of amplitudes.
The central progression is:
A STATE → A POINT ON A SPHERE → HEIGHT DECIDES THE PROBABILITIES → THE ANGLE AROUND THE EQUATOR IS PHASE → THE PICTURE HAS LIMITS
The learner should understand that the sphere is a map of states, and that where a point sits tells them both the probabilities and the phase.
The key conceptual transition is:
“A state can be pictured as a point on a sphere. How high the point is decides the probabilities. How far around it is carries the phase.”
This section should prepare the learner for Quantum Gates (02.04).
________________________________________
Learning Outcomes
By the end of the section, the learner should be able to:
	Explain that the Bloch sphere is a picture of the state of one qubit, not a physical object the qubit sits in.
	Find |0⟩ and |1⟩ at the poles, and |+⟩, |−⟩, |+i⟩, and |−i⟩ on the equator.
	Read the outcome probabilities from a point's height: certain 0 at the north pole, 50/50 on the equator, certain 1 at the south pole.
	Explain that moving around the equator changes the state but not the Z-basis probabilities.
	Identify |+⟩ and |−⟩ as opposite points on the equator with the same height.
	Describe phase as an angle around the equator.
	Explain that a point on the sphere is a state, never a measurement result.
	State that the sphere pictures a pure state of one qubit, up to a shared overall phase.
	Ask how a state can be moved from one point to another.

Page Structure
1. From a List of Numbers to a Picture
Begin directly from the Qubit Representation page.
Revisit the familiar progression:
A STATE IS WRITTEN AS TWO AMPLITUDES
↓
THE SIGN IS A REAL DIFFERENCE, BUT EASY TO MISS IN A LIST
Then ask:
“How can we picture a state, including its sign, instead of reading a list of numbers?”
Name the sphere readout from Module 01 and say that this page turns the sphere into a tool the learner can use to read a state.
Do not repeat Module 01's bare polar-angle and phase-angle sliders as the page's content.
The learner should see the sphere as a picture of the numbers they already know rather than as a new object.

2. A Map of States
Introduce the sphere as a globe-like picture of the states of one qubit.
Clearly distinguish:
	Poles — |0⟩ at the north pole (certain 0) and |1⟩ at the south pole (certain 1).
	Equator — |+⟩, |−⟩, |+i⟩, and |−i⟩, each equally likely to give 0 or 1.
	Point — one state. Not an outcome.
Show a six-row table of the named points with their polar and phase angles.
Locate |0⟩ and |+⟩ first, as the page's recurring examples.
Say plainly that the sphere is a map of states, not a place where the qubit physically sits.
Do not imply that the axes are directions in the room.
Include a short explanation such as:
“Is the qubit pointing up?”
“No. The sphere is a picture of the state. North is not a direction in the room; it is where the state |0⟩ is drawn.”

3. Main Interactive — “Sphere Explorer”
Make this the primary V1 interaction.
Allow the learner to move a point on the sphere and watch the state description and the probability bars.
At minimum support:
	A polar-angle slider from 0° to 180° and a phase-angle slider from 0° to 360°, each with a numeric readout and a typed value.
	Preset buttons for the six named states, taken from QedQubit.PRESETS.
	A “Pin this state” button, and a way to clear the pin.
	Resetting the view to its starting state.
When the learner moves the point:
	Draw the sphere with the labelled named points, the axes, and the dashed projection onto the vertical axis, through QedBloch.render.
	Show the state description: the two magnitudes as decimals and the phase as an angle in degrees, with no complex notation.
	Show the probability bars for outcome 0 and outcome 1, each with its percentage.
	Show a one-line caption in words, such as “Height: halfway, so 50/50. Phase: 90°.”
	Show the sphere, the state description, and the bars in three separate places.
When the state is at a pole:
	Say that the phase angle does not matter there, since the state is |0⟩ or |1⟩ whatever the angle. Use the phiDefined flag from QedQubit.toBloch. Do not show a phase as if it meant something.
When the learner pins a state:
	Leave a faded ghost marker with a different shape and its own label. Keep the live point separate.
	Let the learner move the live point and compare. Clearing the pin or resetting removes it.
The learner should visually experience:
MOVE THE POINT
↓
SEE THE HEIGHT, THE PHASE, AND THE BARS UPDATE
↓
READ WHAT THE STATE IS
Reinforce the distinction established in 02.01 and 02.02:
A state is what was prepared; the point and its numbers are two ways to describe it.
Make the interaction resettable and allow learners to experiment with every preset and every angle.
Pass a neutral color to QedBloch.render so Module 02 does not use Module 07's meanings, and leave Module 01's look unchanged.
At Intermediate and above, as a level-aware behavior gated through QedLevel:
	Show the complex amplitudes from QedQubit.format(QedQubit.fromBloch(θ, φ)).
	Show (x, y, z) from QedQubit.blochVector, and P(0) = (1 + z)/2 beside the bars.
	Add an overall-phase slider that changes the displayed amplitudes but not the point, using QedQubit.applyGlobalPhase, and show that the bars and the point do not change.
The Beginner behavior must never depend on the level.
Make the interaction keyboard operable. Give the canvas role="img" and an aria-label that updates with the state through QedBloch.formatText. Give the same information in a text readout and the named-points table. Summarize the state in an aria-live region.
The sliders and presets are the non-3D alternative. Do not require dragging on the canvas.
Do not add gates, rotations as operations, drag on the canvas, measurement, or shots here.

4. Height Decides the Probabilities
Reconnect to 02.02: the probabilities come from squaring the amplitudes.
Show that the height of the point sets the outcome probabilities.
The learner should understand:
HEIGHT
↓
THE PROBABILITY OF EACH OUTCOME
Walk through three heights:
	North pole — certain 0.
	Equator — 50/50.
	South pole — certain 1.
Show how the probabilities slide smoothly in between.
Show the dashed projection to the vertical axis, and label the poles with their outcome and the equator with “50/50”.
Do not show a formula at Beginner level.
Include three checks, each with immediate explanatory feedback that explains every wrong choice:
	A prediction task: a state sits on the equator; predict the probability of outcome 0 (50%), then compare with the bars.
	A conceptual question: which of several pairs of points give the same Z-basis probabilities? Any two points at the same height do, for example |+⟩ and |−⟩. |0⟩ and |1⟩ do not.
	A transfer task: make outcome 1 happen 25% of the time. A polar angle of 60° works with any phase. The feedback notes that the phase does not matter.

5. Around the Equator: Phase as an Angle
Show that moving around the equator keeps the height, so the probabilities do not change, but the state does.
Compare |+⟩ and |−⟩ with a pinned ghost: same height, opposite sides.
Introduce the term:
Phase — the angle around the equator.
Connect this directly to 02.02:
The real-amplitude states the learner built sit on one circle, where the phase is 0° or 180°.
The learner should discover that two different states can sit at the same height and give identical Z-basis counts.
Ask:
“If the height is the same, in what way are |+⟩ and |−⟩ different?”
Answer only that they sit at different places around the equator, and that later pages show how to tell them apart.
Say in one sentence that the sphere pictures a pure state of one qubit, up to a shared overall phase.
Say in one sentence that a later page shows an operation that turns a phase difference into a difference you can see. Do not name or show a gate.
Do not claim that anything distinguishes them on this page.
Do not teach gates, interference, or how to move a point yet.

6. Beyond the Core
Build the heading inside the Intermediate block, as on 02.01 and 02.02, so a Beginner sees neither an empty heading nor a table-of-contents entry for it.
[Intermediate] The Formal Picture
Add one gated block that covers:
	The formula |ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + e^{iφ} sin(θ/2)|1⟩ and how θ and φ relate to the amplitudes.
	The Bloch vector (x, y, z), and P(0) = (1 + z)/2 as the height seen through the formula.
	Global phase: descriptions that differ by an overall phase are the same point, shown with the overall-phase control.
	Opposite points as orthogonal states, with a link to the Orthogonal States entry. Use QedQubit.innerProduct to show ⟨ψ|φ⟩ = 0 for an opposite pair.
	The limits of the picture, named and not taught: several qubits do not fit on one sphere, and mixed states do not sit on its surface.
Explain the idea visually, through the Sphere Explorer's readouts, rather than relying on the equations.
Do not state the general Born rule. It belongs to 02.07.
Do not teach tensor products. They belong to 02.05.
[Advanced] Naming the Formalism
Add a very short gated block naming mixed states inside the ball and the density-matrix view, with no formalism.
Build both as gated blocks as described in @llm/CONVENTIONS.md section 7.

7. From Picture to Motion
End by comparing what the learner can do now with what is still unknown.
So far:
READ A STATE AS A POINT
↓
READ THE PROBABILITIES FROM THE HEIGHT
↓
READ THE PHASE FROM THE ANGLE
versus:
GET A STATE TO A DIFFERENT POINT
↓
CHANGE THE STATE ON PURPOSE
↓
BUILD THE STATES WE NEED
Present a final example with the learner's own pinned point and a second point somewhere else on the sphere.
Ask:
“How can we move a state from one point to another?”
Then introduce the next problem:
A point can sit anywhere on the sphere, but we do not yet know how to get it from one place to another.
Do not explain gates or rotations here.
End with:
“How can we move a state from one point to another?”

Important Pedagogical Constraints
Do not imply:
A POINT ON THE SPHERE = A MEASUREMENT RESULT
Instead teach:
The point is the state. A measurement returns 0 or 1.
Do not imply:
THE SPHERE = PHYSICAL SPACE
Instead teach:
The sphere is a map of states. The qubit is not an arrow pointing somewhere in the room, and nothing here is a physical rotation.
Do not imply:
PHASE CHANGES THE Z-BASIS PROBABILITIES
Instead teach:
Moving around the equator keeps the height, so the probabilities do not change, but the state does.
Do not imply that north pole means up in any physical sense. It is the state |0⟩.
Do not imply that |+⟩ and |−⟩ can be told apart by Z-basis counts, or that this page shows how to tell them apart.
Do not imply that every state of every system fits on this sphere. Say plainly that it pictures one qubit in a pure state, up to a shared overall phase.
Do not imply that the sphere's axes are the three coordinate directions of the lab.
Do not teach gates, how to move a point, tensor products, entanglement, the Born rule as a formula, physical rotations, noise, or decoherence here.
Keep Beginner V1 focused on the mental model required to understand why a state needs to be moved.

End State
The learner should leave with:
A STATE
↓
A POINT ON A SPHERE
↓
HEIGHT SETS THE PROBABILITIES, AND THE ANGLE AROUND THE EQUATOR IS PHASE
↓
THE PICTURE SHOWS ONE QUBIT IN A PURE STATE
And understand the central relationship:
State → point → height gives the probabilities, angle gives the phase.
The central takeaway should be:
A qubit's state can be pictured as a point on a sphere. Height sets the probabilities and the angle around the equator carries the phase, and the picture shows one qubit in a pure state only.
End with:
“How can we move a state from one point to another?”
