## Open questions for review

Each has a recommendation. Say which you accept or change; the rest of the draft already assumes the recommendations.

1. **Where does the builder's code live?** The taxonomy says "reuse `QedCircuit`; the builder extends it". The builder needs three things: a circuit as plain data that can be edited and checked, a way to run that data through the state math, and a drawing. *Recommendation:* extend **`QedCircuit`** with the data layer only, with no new dependencies: `emptyCircuit`, `place`, `remove` (pure, returning a new circuit), `validate` (returns the problems in plain words), and `columns`. Put the running in a **new small module `QedTrace` (`trace.js`)** whose whole job is composition: `run(circuit, initial)` applies each column through `QedGates` and `QedTwoQubit` and returns the state after every step. It is the one shared module that calls others, so `tests/js/load.js` gains a `loadModules` helper that evaluates several modules against one stub window. The Bell drawing in Module 05 must stay byte-identical (the existing hash test already pins it).

2. **How many wires and columns?** *Recommendation:* **always two wires, q0 on top and q1 below, and up to five columns.** A single-qubit circuit just leaves q1 unused (it stays in |0⟩ and is shown that way). Five columns is room for the Bell circuit (two), the H–Z–H challenge (three), and some experimenting. One column is one step. Gates on different wires may share a column, and a CNOT takes both wires of its column.

3. **How do you edit a circuit without dragging?** *Recommendation:* an **accessible grid of cell buttons** (rows are wires, columns are steps). Choose a gate in the palette, then activate a cell to place it, and activate an occupied cell to remove it. The `QedCircuit` drawing sits beside it as "what you have drawn", in the same format as 02.05 and Module 05. Everything works from the keyboard and nothing requires dragging.

4. **The palette.** The taxonomy asks for the symbols for H, X, Z, CNOT, and measurement. *Recommendation:* exactly those five at Beginner. Intermediate adds Y, S, T, and the three rotation gates RX, RY, and RZ with an angle control, as 02.04's Intermediate view does. The palette is generated from `QedGates.GATES`, not hardcoded.

5. **Which way does a CNOT point?** In 02.05 q0 was the control by convention. *Recommendation:* in a circuit **either wire can be the control**. Placing a CNOT by activating the cell on the control's wire makes the other wire the target, and the drawing shows the dot and the ⊕ accordingly. `QedGates` gains `cnot(controlWire)`, which returns the matrix for either direction, so the trace supports both. This teaches that control and target are roles of the wires, and the page says that 02.05's "q0 is the control" was the convention for that page.

6. **Initial states.** *Recommendation:* each wire starts in **|0⟩ or |1⟩** at Beginner (a circuit conventionally starts from a computational basis state), with |0⟩ the default and shown beside the wire. Intermediate adds the other four named states.

7. **What the trace shows.** The taxonomy asks to trace a circuit "by what the outcome probabilities (or the sphere, or the joint table) look like after each gate". *Recommendation:* after every column, show the **joint probability bars**, the **four amplitudes as a plain labelled list** (real numbers, for listing only, as on 02.05), and a **name for the state when it has one** (`QedQubit.nameOf` for each wire of a product state, `QedTwoQubit.bellOf` for a Bell state, and a plain "an entangled state" otherwise). The names matter: in H, Z, H the probabilities look identical after H and after Z, and only the names (|+⟩, then |−⟩) show that Z did something. No Bloch spheres (02.05 decided against them for two qubits, and the trace must work for both).

8. **What the measurement symbol does.** *Recommendation:* the meter symbol is allowed only as the **last thing on its wire** (nothing may follow it on that wire, and a CNOT may not follow a measurement on a wire it uses). The trace ends there and labels the bars "what a measurement here would show: probabilities, not a result". No sampling, no shots, and no state update (02.07). Mid-circuit measurement stays a named idea for 02.07's Advanced block.

9. **The guided goals.** The taxonomy asks for "a guided circuit builder with a limited gate palette, a state trace, validation feedback, and reset". *Recommendation:* four goals, each with a "Check my circuit" button, a hint on request, and a "Show one solution" that loads it: (a) make q0 certain 1 with one gate; (b) make q0 an even mix; (c) make the Bell state, with 00 and 11 each 50%; (d) turn |0⟩ into certain 1 **without using X** (H, then Z, then H). Any circuit that reaches the goal counts. Goal (d) is the transfer task and shows that two different circuits can do the same job; the name HZH = X and the matrix proof are Intermediate.

10. **Reading order and "not physical timing".** The taxonomy says the diagram's direction is a convention, not a guarantee of timing or hardware layout. *Recommendation:* a short subsection of its own. Circuits are read left to right and q0 is the top wire. Steps in the same column act on different wires, so their order does not matter. The diagram says what to do and in what order the maths is applied; a real machine may run steps at overlapping times, reorder independent ones, or compile them differently, which is Module 05's subject. Link to the Module 05 page as a reference only.

11. **Overlap with Module 05.** Module 05.01 already draws the Bell-state circuit and says a circuit is an instruction, not a state. *Recommendation:* open by naming that drawing, reuse the same one, and do not repeat the software-stack material. No SDK, code, transpilation, or backend content (Modules 03 and 05).

12. **What Intermediate adds.** *Recommendation:* two gated blocks, plus level-aware behavior in the builder. Block one is *Tracing with amplitudes*: the trace shows the four-component vector after each column, the matrices of the steps (H on q0 as H⊗I, CNOT in either direction), and the reading-order reminder (diagram left to right, matrix product right to left). Block two is *Parameters and identities*: rotation gates with an angle in a circuit, and the identities HZH = X and HXH = Z, shown first by tracing and then by multiplying the matrices. "Code export" is named as later and not built. The builder gains the extra palette items, extra initial states, and the vector and matrix readouts. Advanced names circuit depth, circuit identities as a tool, and Clifford against non-Clifford gates.

13. **A teaser for measurement.** *Recommendation:* close by asking how the probabilities the trace shows become the results we actually see, with one sentence and no shots, no histogram, and no mention of state update. Next: **Measurement →**

## Goal

Teach the learner to read and draw a quantum circuit, and to trace what each step does to the outcome probabilities, building on the steps they have been describing in words and tables.

Central progression:

`STEPS IN WORDS → A DIAGRAM ON WIRES → READ LEFT TO RIGHT → TRACE THE STATE STEP BY STEP → BUILD TO A GOAL → THE DIAGRAM IS A CONVENTION, NOT A CLOCK`

Key conceptual transition: **"A circuit is a diagram of steps. Read it from left to right, and at every step the state and its probabilities change. The diagram says what to do, not when a machine does it."**

Builds on 02.05's closing question: how can we write these steps down as a diagram? Ends by asking how the probabilities a trace shows turn into results (02.07).

## Learning outcomes

**Beginner (the Core Learning Contract).** The learner should be able to:

1. Name the parts of a circuit diagram: wires, initial states, gates in columns, and the measurement symbol.
2. Read a small circuit from left to right, with q0 on the top wire.
3. Recognize the symbols for H, X, Z, CNOT (control and target), and measurement.
4. Trace a circuit by the joint outcome probabilities, and by the name of the state, after each step.
5. Explain why H on q0 followed by CNOT turns |00⟩ into the Bell state, in terms of the joint outcomes at each step.
6. Draw an elementary circuit, including the Bell-state circuit, from a goal.
7. Explain that the order in a diagram is a reading convention and not physical timing, and that steps in the same column do not depend on each other.
8. Explain that a measurement symbol closes a wire in a circuit, and that a trace shows probabilities and not a result.
9. Recognize that different circuits can do the same job.
10. Ask how the probabilities a circuit produces turn into the results we observe.

**Intermediate (gated).** Trace a circuit at the amplitude level, with two-qubit vectors; use rotation gates with parameters in a circuit; check the identities HZH = X and HXH = Z by tracing and by matrix multiplication.

**Advanced (gated).** Name circuit depth, circuit identities as a tool, and Clifford against non-Clifford gates.

## Experience-level scope

**Beginner (V1) teaches:** wires, initial states, and columns; the left-to-right reading convention and q0 on top; the symbols for H, X, Z, CNOT, and measurement; reading small circuits; tracing by probabilities and state names; the Bell-state circuit traced step by step; building small circuits to a goal, with checks; the diagram order as a convention and not timing; a measurement symbol as the end of a wire.

**Intermediate (gated now):** amplitude-level tracing with two-qubit vectors; the matrices of the steps; rotation gates with parameters; the identities HZH = X and HXH = Z; the extra palette and initial states in the builder.

**Advanced (named only):** circuit depth; identities as a tool; Clifford and non-Clifford gates.

**Not on this page (any level):** SDK setup, code, transpilation, backend selection, and runtime (Modules 03 and 05), shots, histograms, the Born rule, and the state update after a measurement (02.07), mid-circuit measurement and classical control, three or more qubits, other named two-qubit gates, hardware timing, noise, and decoherence (Module 04).

## Page structure (draft)

1. **From a Table to a Diagram.** Reconnect to 02.05: the Bell state was made by steps described in words and tables. Ask: "How can we write these steps down as a diagram?" Name Module 05's drawing of the same circuit and say that a circuit is an instruction, not a state.

2. **Reading a Circuit.** Introduce wires (one per qubit, q0 on top), initial states written beside the wires, gates in columns, and the measurement symbol. The symbols for H, X, Z, and CNOT (a dot on the control wire joined to a ⊕ on the target), drawn through `QedCircuit` and taken from `QedGates`. Read left to right. One worked reading: "H on q0, then CNOT with q0 as control".

3. **Tracing a Circuit.** The Bell circuit traced column by column: |00⟩ (100% for 00), after H (50% for 00 and 50% for 10), after CNOT (50% for 00 and 50% for 11). Explain why in the terms of 02.05: H made q0 an even mix, and CNOT copied the choice onto q1 by flipping it on the part where q0 is 1. The state has a name at each step.

4. **Main Interactive: "Circuit Builder."** An accessible grid on two wires and five columns, a palette, per-wire initial states, a live trace, validation feedback, four guided goals, and reset. See below.

5. **What the Diagram Does Not Say.** The direction is a convention. Left to right is the reading order, and q0 on top is a convention. Gates in the same column act on different wires, so their order does not matter. A real machine may run steps at overlapping times, reorder independent steps, or compile them differently (Module 05, referenced only). The diagram describes what to do; it does not describe time or layout.

6. **Beyond the Core.** Gated blocks (heading inside the Intermediate block, as on 02.01 to 02.05).
   - `[Intermediate]` *Tracing with amplitudes.* The four-component vector after each column and the matrix of each step, with the reading-order reminder.
   - `[Intermediate]` *Parameters and identities.* Rotation gates with an angle in a circuit, and HZH = X and HXH = Z by tracing and by matrices. Code export named as later.
   - `[Advanced]` A very short block naming circuit depth, circuit identities as a tool, and Clifford against non-Clifford gates.

7. **From Circuits to Measurement.** Bridge. A circuit can end with measurement symbols, and the trace shows the probabilities of the outcomes, but a probability is not a result. Ask: "How do these probabilities turn into the results we actually see?" Next: **Measurement →**

## Main Interactive: "Circuit Builder"

- **Controls:**
  - an initial state for each wire (|0⟩ or |1⟩ at Beginner);
  - a palette: H, X, Z, CNOT, and measure (Beginner), generated from `QedGates.GATES`;
  - a grid of cell buttons, rows for the wires (q₀ on top) and columns for the steps (five), to place the chosen gate or remove an occupied cell;
  - "Check my circuit", "Show a hint", "Show one solution", and reset, for the current goal;
  - a goal selector for the four goals (question 9), and a free-play option with no goal.
- **Display, kept in separate places:**
  - the **circuit drawing** through `QedCircuit` (neutral gate boxes), with each wire's initial state named beside it;
  - the **trace**: one row per step (the start, then after each column), each with a label such as "after step 2: CNOT", the **joint probability bars**, the **four amplitudes** as a plain labelled list, and the **name of the state** when it has one;
  - the **validation message**, in words and with an icon;
  - the **goal and its check result**, with a hint when asked.
- **Validation (checked as the learner builds, never silent):**
  - a column holds one operation per wire, and a CNOT uses both wires of its column;
  - a CNOT's control and target are different wires;
  - a measurement is the last thing on its wire, and a CNOT cannot follow a measurement on a wire it uses;
  - the circuit has at most five columns.
  An invalid placement is refused with a plain sentence saying why ("A CNOT needs both wires in its column, and q₁ already has a gate there"), not corrected.
- **Reading it in words:** a one-line summary of the circuit, such as "H on q₀, then CNOT with control q₀ and target q₁, then a measurement of both".
- **Intermediate behavior (level-aware):** Y, S, T, and the rotation gates (with an angle slider and a typed value) join the palette; the other four named states become initial states; the trace shows the four-component vector and the matrix of the step. The Beginner behavior never depends on the level, and dropping to Beginner with a circuit that uses an Intermediate gate clears it with a note.
- **Accessibility:** the grid, the palette, and the checks are keyboard-operable and need no pointer. Each cell has a name ("q₀, step 2, empty" or "q₀, step 2, H"). The circuit has a text description, every bar has a text percentage, and an aria-live region announces each placement, each refusal, and the check result.
- **Scope fence:** no code export, no SDK, no shots or sampling, no mid-circuit measurement, no third wire, no other gates, no Bloch spheres.

### Guided goals and checks (taxonomy section 7)

- *Prediction task:* "Start in |00⟩. The circuit is X on q0, then CNOT. Predict the joint probabilities" (certain 11), then compare with the trace. Feedback explains that X made q0 a 1, so CNOT flipped q1.
- *Conceptual question:* "In the circuit H then Z on one wire, which gate acts first?" (H.) Feedback explains each choice, including that the diagram order is the order the maths is applied, not a clock.
- *Transfer task:* turn |0⟩ into certain 1 without using X (H, Z, H), checked in the builder. The feedback notes that the middle of the trace looked unchanged in probability but not in name, and that two different circuits did the same job.

## Important pedagogical constraints (draft)

Do not imply:
- `THE DIAGRAM = WHAT THE MACHINE DOES IN TIME`. Left to right is the reading order. A real machine may overlap, reorder, or compile steps.
- `A CIRCUIT = A STATE`. A circuit is an instruction. The state is what it produces from an initial state. (Module 05 makes the same point.)
- `THE TRACE = A MEASUREMENT RESULT`. The trace shows probabilities. A measurement returns one outcome.
- `PROBABILITIES UNCHANGED = NOTHING HAPPENED`. In H, Z, H the step with Z leaves the probabilities alone and changes the state.
- That q0 must always be the control, or that q0 on top is a law. Both are conventions of this module, and other tools may order wires and bits differently.
- That there is only one circuit for a job. Different circuits can give the same result.
- That a measurement in the middle of a circuit is covered here. The symbol closes a wire on this page.
- That this page shows how circuits are written in code or run on hardware.

Do not teach here: code or SDKs, transpilation, backends, runtime, shots, the Born rule, the state update after a measurement, mid-circuit measurement, three or more qubits, other named two-qubit gates, hardware timing, noise, or decoherence.

## End state (draft)

The learner leaves with: `STEPS ON WIRES → READ LEFT TO RIGHT → TRACE THE STATE AND ITS PROBABILITIES AT EACH STEP → BUILD TO A GOAL → THE DIAGRAM IS A CONVENTION, NOT A CLOCK`.

Takeaway: *A circuit is a diagram of steps on wires. Read it from left to right, trace the state and its probabilities after each step, and remember that the diagram says what to do, not when a machine does it.*
