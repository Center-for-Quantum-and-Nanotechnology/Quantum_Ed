This section continues Module 02 — Quantum Computing Fundamentals. 02.05 made the Bell state with steps described in words and tables. This page writes those steps down as a diagram and traces them.
________________________________________
Goal
Teach the learner to read and draw a quantum circuit, and to trace what each step does to the outcome probabilities, building on the steps they have been describing in words and tables.
The central progression is:
STEPS IN WORDS → A DIAGRAM ON WIRES → READ LEFT TO RIGHT → TRACE THE STATE STEP BY STEP → BUILD TO A GOAL → THE DIAGRAM IS A CONVENTION, NOT A CLOCK
The learner should understand that a circuit is an instruction, and that tracing it shows what state and probabilities each step produces.
The key conceptual transition is:
“A circuit is a diagram of steps. Read it from left to right, and at every step the state and its probabilities change. The diagram says what to do, not when a machine does it.”
This section should prepare the learner for Measurement (02.07).
________________________________________
Learning Outcomes
By the end of the section, the learner should be able to:
	Name the parts of a circuit diagram: wires, initial states, gates in columns, and the measurement symbol.
	Read a small circuit from left to right, with q0 on the top wire.
	Recognize the symbols for H, X, Z, CNOT (control and target), and measurement.
	Trace a circuit by the joint outcome probabilities, and by the name of the state, after each step.
	Explain why H on q0 followed by CNOT turns |00⟩ into the Bell state, in terms of the joint outcomes at each step.
	Draw an elementary circuit, including the Bell-state circuit, from a goal.
	Explain that the order in a diagram is a reading convention and not physical timing, and that steps in the same column do not depend on each other.
	Explain that a measurement symbol closes a wire in a circuit, and that a trace shows probabilities and not a result.
	Recognize that different circuits can do the same job.
	Ask how the probabilities a circuit produces turn into the results we observe.

Page Structure
1. From a Table to a Diagram
Begin directly from the Multi-Qubit Systems page.
Revisit the familiar progression:
THE BELL STATE IS MADE BY H ON q0, THEN CNOT
↓
THOSE STEPS HAVE BEEN DESCRIBED IN WORDS AND TABLES
Then ask:
“How can we write these steps down as a diagram?”
Name Module 05's drawing of the same circuit and say that a circuit is an instruction, not a state.
Do not repeat the software-stack material.
The learner should see a circuit as a standard way of writing steps down rather than as a new kind of object.

2. Reading a Circuit
Introduce the parts of a circuit diagram.
Clearly distinguish:
	Wire — one line per qubit, with q0 on top.
	Initial state — what the qubit starts in, written beside its wire.
	Gate — a labelled box on a wire, or a dot joined to a ⊕ for CNOT.
	Column — one step, read from left to right.
	Measurement symbol — a meter, ending its wire.
Show the symbols for H, X, Z, CNOT (a dot on the control wire joined to a ⊕ on the target wire), and measurement, drawn through QedCircuit and taken from QedGates.
Read one circuit aloud: “H on q₀, then CNOT with q₀ as the control and q₁ as the target.”
Do not imply that a circuit is a state.
Include a short explanation such as:
“Is the circuit the state?”
“No. The circuit is the instruction. The state is what the instruction produces from the initial states.”

3. Tracing a Circuit
Reconnect to the joint tables of 02.05.
Trace the Bell circuit column by column, from |00⟩.
The learner should understand:
START: 100% FOR 00
↓
AFTER H: 50% FOR 00 AND 50% FOR 10
↓
AFTER CNOT: 50% FOR 00 AND 50% FOR 11
Show the joint probability bars at every step, with the name of the state when it has one.
Explain why in the terms of 02.05: H made q0 an even mix, and CNOT flipped q1 on the part of the state where q0 is 1.
Say that tracing is something we do to the circuit, and that the page is showing probabilities, not results.
Do not teach how the outcome is observed.

4. Main Interactive — “Circuit Builder”
Make this the primary V1 interaction.
Allow the learner to build a small circuit on two wires, and watch the state trace.
At minimum support:
	An initial state for each wire: |0⟩ or |1⟩, with |0⟩ the default.
	A palette of H, X, Z, CNOT, and measure, generated from QedGates.GATES.
	A grid of cell buttons, with rows for the wires (q₀ on top) and five columns for the steps, to place the chosen gate or to remove an occupied cell.
	CNOT placed by activating the cell on the control's wire, so that either wire can be the control and the other is the target.
	A goal selector for four goals, and a free-play option with no goal.
	“Check my circuit”, “Show a hint”, “Show one solution”, and a reset.
When the learner places or removes a gate, or changes an initial state:
	Show the circuit drawing through QedCircuit with neutral gate boxes, with each wire's initial state named beside it.
	Show the trace: one row for the start and one after each column, each with a label such as “after step 2: CNOT”, the joint probability bars, the four amplitudes as a plain labelled list of real numbers for listing only, and the name of the state when it has one.
	Name a state with QedQubit.nameOf for each wire of a product state, with QedTwoQubit.bellOf for a Bell state, and otherwise as “an entangled state”.
	Show a one-line summary in words, such as “H on q₀, then CNOT with control q₀ and target q₁, then a measurement of both.”
	Show the drawing, the trace, the validation message, and the goal result in separate places.
When the learner makes a placement that is not allowed:
	Refuse it in a plain sentence with an icon, such as “A CNOT needs both wires in its column, and q₁ already has a gate there.” Do not correct it silently.
	Apply these rules: a column holds one operation per wire, and a CNOT uses both wires of its column; a CNOT's control and target are different wires; a measurement is the last thing on its wire, and a CNOT cannot follow a measurement on a wire it uses; the circuit has at most five columns.
When the circuit has a measurement symbol:
	End the trace there, and label the bars “what a measurement here would show: probabilities, not a result”.
	Do not sample, show counts, or update the state.
When the learner chooses a goal:
	Offer these four: (a) make q0 certain 1 with one gate; (b) make q0 an even mix; (c) make the Bell state, with 00 and 11 each 50%; (d) turn |0⟩ into certain 1 without using X.
	Count any circuit that reaches the goal. Check goals by the final joint probabilities and, for (d), that the circuit has no X.
	Give the hint on request, and load one solution on request: (a) X; (b) H; (c) H on q₀ then CNOT; (d) H, Z, H.
The learner should visually experience:
PLACE GATES ON WIRES
↓
SEE THE STATE AND ITS PROBABILITIES CHANGE AFTER EACH COLUMN
↓
CHECK THE CIRCUIT AGAINST A GOAL
Reinforce the distinction established on 02.05:
A circuit is an instruction; the trace is the state and the probabilities it produces.
Make the interaction resettable and allow learners to experiment with every placement.
At Intermediate and above, as a level-aware behavior gated through QedLevel:
	Add Y, S, T, and the rotation gates RX, RY, and RZ to the palette, with an angle slider and a typed value.
	Add the other four named states as initial states.
	Show the four-component vector and the matrix of the step in the trace.
The Beginner behavior must never depend on the level. When the learner drops to Beginner with a circuit that uses an Intermediate gate or state, clear it and say so.
Make the interaction keyboard operable, with no pointer needed. Give each cell a name such as “q₀, step 2, H” or “q₀, step 2, empty”. Give the circuit a text description, pair every bar with a text percentage, and announce each placement, refusal, and check result in an aria-live region.
Do not add code export, an SDK, shots or sampling, mid-circuit measurement, a third wire, other gates, or Bloch spheres here.
Include three checks with immediate explanatory feedback that explains every wrong choice:
	A prediction task: start in |00⟩, with the circuit X on q₀ then CNOT. Predict the joint probabilities (certain 11), then compare with the trace. The feedback explains that X made q₀ a 1, so CNOT flipped q₁.
	A conceptual question: in the circuit H then Z on one wire, which gate acts first? The answer is H. The feedback explains each choice, including that the diagram order is the order the maths is applied and not a clock.
	A transfer task: turn |0⟩ into certain 1 without using X, checked in the builder as goal (d). The feedback notes that the middle of the trace looked unchanged in probability but not in name, and that two different circuits did the same job.

5. What the Diagram Does Not Say
Make the distinction explicit.
Reading order
Circuits are read from left to right, with q0 on the top wire. These are conventions of this module.
Physical time
The diagram says what to do and in what order the maths is applied. It does not say when a machine does each step.
Use the builder to show that:
Two gates in the same column act on different wires, so their order does not matter.
Say that a real machine may run steps at overlapping times, reorder independent ones, or compile them differently. That is the subject of Module 05, referenced here and not taught.
Say that other tools may order wires and bit strings differently.
Do not imply that the diagram is a timeline.

6. Beyond the Core
Build the heading inside the Intermediate block, as on 02.01 to 02.05, so a Beginner sees neither an empty heading nor a table-of-contents entry for it.
[Intermediate] Tracing with Amplitudes
Add a gated block that covers:
	The four-component vector after each column, for the Bell circuit and for the circuits in the builder.
	The matrix of each step: H on q₀ as H⊗I, and CNOT in either direction.
	The reading-order reminder: a diagram reads left to right, and a matrix product reads right to left.
[Intermediate] Parameters and Identities
Add a second gated block after the first, covering:
	Rotation gates with an angle, used in a circuit.
	The identities HZH = X and HXH = Z, shown first by tracing and then by multiplying the matrices.
	A note that code export is a later addition, not part of this page.
Explain the ideas visually, through the builder's trace and matrix readouts, rather than relying on the equations.
Do not state the general Born rule. It belongs to 02.07.
Do not teach three or more qubits or other named two-qubit gates here.
[Advanced] Naming the Formalism
Add a very short gated block naming circuit depth, circuit identities as a tool, and Clifford against non-Clifford gates, with no formalism.
Build all three as gated blocks as described in @llm/CONVENTIONS.md section 7.

7. From Circuits to Measurement
End by comparing what the learner can do now with what is still unknown.
So far:
WRITE THE STEPS AS A CIRCUIT
↓
READ AND BUILD ONE
↓
TRACE THE PROBABILITIES AT EACH STEP
versus:
SEE WHAT A MEASUREMENT RETURNS
↓
SEE A RESULT RATHER THAN A PROBABILITY
↓
SEE WHAT HAPPENS TO THE STATE AFTERWARDS
Present a final example with the Bell circuit ending in two measurement symbols, with its probabilities and no counts.
Ask:
“How do these probabilities turn into the results we actually see?”
Then introduce the next problem:
A trace shows what a measurement would give. A measurement gives one outcome at a time, and we have not yet looked at what happens when we repeat it.
Do not explain shots, the Born rule, or the state update here.
End with:
“How do these probabilities turn into the results we actually see?”

Important Pedagogical Constraints
Do not imply:
THE DIAGRAM = WHAT THE MACHINE DOES IN TIME
Instead teach:
Left to right is the reading order. A real machine may overlap, reorder, or compile steps.
Do not imply:
A CIRCUIT = A STATE
Instead teach:
A circuit is an instruction. The state is what it produces from the initial states.
Do not imply:
THE TRACE = A MEASUREMENT RESULT
Instead teach:
The trace shows probabilities. A measurement returns one outcome.
Do not imply that unchanged probabilities mean nothing happened. In H, Z, H the step with Z leaves the probabilities alone and changes the state.
Do not imply that q0 must always be the control, or that q0 on top is a law. Both are conventions of this module, and other tools may order wires and bits differently.
Do not imply that there is only one circuit for a job. Different circuits can give the same result.
Do not imply that a measurement in the middle of a circuit is covered here. The symbol closes a wire on this page.
Do not imply that this page shows how circuits are written in code or run on hardware.
Do not teach code or SDKs, transpilation, backends, runtime, shots, the Born rule, the state update after a measurement, mid-circuit measurement, three or more qubits, other named two-qubit gates, hardware timing, noise, or decoherence here.
Keep Beginner V1 focused on the mental model required to understand why a circuit's probabilities need a measurement to become results.
