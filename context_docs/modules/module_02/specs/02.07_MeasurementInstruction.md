This section closes Module 02 — Quantum Computing Fundamentals. 02.06 ended with circuits whose meters showed probabilities and no results. This page makes the meters return results, and shows what a measurement does to the state.
________________________________________
Goal
Teach the learner what a measurement returns and what it does, building on circuits that end in meters whose probabilities they can already trace.
The central progression is:
PROBABILITIES FROM THE STATE → ONE MEASUREMENT GIVES ONE RESULT → THE STATE NOW MATCHES IT → MANY SHOTS SHOW THE DISTRIBUTION → PHASE SHOWS UP AFTER AN H → A PAIR AGREES WITHOUT A MESSAGE
The learner should understand that a state sets the probabilities, a measurement draws one result from them, and only many shots show the probabilities.
The key conceptual transition is:
“A state gives probabilities. A measurement gives one result, drawn from them, and afterwards the state matches the result. Only many measurements of identically prepared states show the probabilities, and one kind of measurement does not show everything about the state.”
This section should prepare the learner for Quantum Programming (Module 03).
________________________________________
Learning Outcomes
By the end of the section, the learner should be able to:
	Say what a computational-basis measurement returns: one 0 or 1 for a qubit, one two-bit result for a pair, drawn with probabilities.
	Compute the probabilities from real amplitudes by squaring, and check that they add to 1.
	Predict the theoretical distribution for the state a short circuit prepares.
	Distinguish a probability from a finite-shot frequency, and explain why counts differ from the prediction and look closer to it with more shots.
	Explain that one shot cannot reveal the amplitudes or the state.
	Describe the ideal state update: after a measurement, the state matches the outcome seen, so measuring again gives the same result.
	Explain that a measurement is not a gate: it gives a result and cannot be undone by a later gate.
	Explain why a phase difference cannot be seen by a direct Z-basis measurement but changes the result after an H.
	Predict the Bell-pair outcomes (00 and 11 about half each, 01 and 10 never), and explain that each qubit alone looks like a fair coin, the agreement appears only when the results are compared, and the measurements do not send a message to each other.
	Name what this ideal model leaves out (real readout, noise, and bit-order differences in tools), and ask how circuits are written and run.

Page Structure
1. From Probabilities to Results
Begin directly from the Quantum Circuits page.
Revisit the familiar progression:
THE CIRCUIT ENDS IN METERS
↓
THE TRACE SHOWS WHAT A MEASUREMENT WOULD GIVE, AS PROBABILITIES
Then ask:
“What does a measurement give, and what happens to the state afterwards?”
Name 02.01's demo, which drew outcomes from a prepared state, and Module 01's repeated-trial and CHSH demos. Say what is new here: predicted against sampled, the update after a measurement, phase made visible by an H, and a pair measured together.
Do not repeat either earlier demo, and do not repeat the CHSH game.
Define a computational-basis measurement as “read the qubit as 0 or 1”.
The learner should see a measurement as the step that turns a state into an ordinary result rather than as another gate.

2. One Measurement: A Result, Then an Update
Introduce one measurement in two parts.
A result:
Show that the probabilities are the squares of the real amplitudes (|+⟩: 0.707² ≈ 0.5; the mostly-0 state: 0.866² ≈ 0.75 and 0.5² = 0.25), and that one measurement returns one outcome with those chances.
Clearly distinguish, as on 02.01:
	State — what the preparation produced.
	Probability — how likely each outcome is, set by the state.
	Outcome — what one measurement returned.
Do not imply that one measurement reveals the state.
Include a short explanation such as:
“Can one measurement tell you the state?”
“No. It gives one result, and many different states could have given it.”
An update:
In the ideal model, after the measurement the state matches the outcome that was seen, so measuring again gives the same result.
State this as the rule of the ideal textbook model, not as a description of how a device works.
Contrast it with a gate: a gate moves a state and can be undone (02.04); a measurement gives a result, and no later gate undoes it.
The learner should understand:
STATE SETS THE PROBABILITIES
↓
ONE MEASUREMENT DRAWS ONE RESULT
↓
THE STATE NOW MATCHES THAT RESULT
Do not require complex amplitudes or the word “projection” at Beginner level.

3. Many Shots
Reconnect to 02.01's shots.
Introduce a shot as one prepare-and-measure.
Explain conceptually:
A probability describes the state. A frequency describes one finite run.
Counts rarely match the prediction exactly, and a larger number of shots tends to look closer to it.
Say that one shot cannot reveal the amplitudes.
Ask:
“How many shots would you need to tell a 50% state from a 55% state?”
Leave the question open for the learner to explore in the lab.
Do not imply that a gap between counts and the prediction is an error.
Do not give the 1/√N rule at Beginner level.

4. Main Interactive — “Measurement Lab”
Make this the primary V1 interaction.
Allow the learner to prepare a state with a short circuit, predict what a measurement will show, and measure it once or many times.
At minimum support:
	A switch between One qubit and A Bell pair.
	For one qubit, a source, shown as the circuit that prepares it from |0⟩: nothing (|0⟩), X (|1⟩), H (|+⟩), H then Z (|−⟩), and rotate by 60° about y (the mostly-0 state, 75% and 25%).
	A toggle, “Add an H before measuring”, that adds one gate to the circuit (one qubit).
	For a pair, the Bell circuit (H on q₀, then CNOT) and a switch for which qubit to measure first.
	A shot count of 1, 10, 100, or 1000, with “Measure”, “Run again”, and reset.
	“Measure once”, a stepper with three panels, and “Measure the same qubit again”.
	A Mystery state challenge.
When the learner chooses a source or toggles the H:
	Draw the circuit through QedCircuit with neutral gate boxes, ending in meters, with the sources run through QedTrace.
	Show the prediction in its own place: the state's name, its real amplitudes as a plain list with their squares, and the probability bars, labelled “predicted”.
	In one-qubit mode, leave q₁ idle and not shown, as on 02.06.
When the learner measures with a shot count:
	Draw the shots from a seeded QedShots session, so the first run is the same on every load and “Run again” gives new results.
	Show the sampled result in its own place, in the same layout as the prediction: counts and percentages, labelled “sampled”, with one plain line such as “Expected about 50 for each; got 56 and 44”.
	Label every result “simulated: ideal and noiseless”.
	Say in words that the prediction describes the state and the counts describe this run.
	For a pair, show the joint counts and, beside them, the two single-qubit counts.
When the learner chooses “Measure once”:
	Show BEFORE: the state's name, its amplitudes, and its probability bars.
	Show OUTCOME: one result, drawn with the seeded random number, as a single labelled token.
	Show AFTER: the state now matches the outcome, so the probability of that outcome is 100%.
	Offer “Measure the same qubit again”, which gives the same result every time.
	For a pair, measure the chosen qubit first, then show the second result as certain, equal to the first.
When the learner starts the Mystery state challenge:
	Hide the source, let the learner choose the shot count, and let them measure with or without an H.
	Run two fixed rounds, in a fixed order: round 1 asks which of three states it is (mostly 0, balanced, mostly 1); round 2 asks whether it is |+⟩ or |−⟩.
	Report the actual counts in the feedback, and explain why more shots or an H settles the question.
The learner should visually experience:
PREPARE A STATE AND CHOOSE THE SHOTS
↓
THE STATE AND ITS PREDICTED PROBABILITIES
↓
THE SAMPLED COUNTS, AND THE SINGLE OUTCOMES WITH THEIR UPDATES
Reinforce the distinction established on 02.01:
A state is not an outcome, and a probability is not a count.
Make the interaction resettable and allow learners to experiment with every source, toggle, and shot count.
At Intermediate and above, as a level-aware behavior gated through QedLevel:
	Add the complex sources |+i⟩ and |−i⟩.
	Add a measurement-basis selector (Z, X, Y) that inserts the basis-change gates (H for X; S-inverse then H for Y), draws them in the circuit, and relabels the outcomes (“+” and “−” for X).
	Show the expectation value.
	Add a spread view that repeats the experiment many times at a chosen N, draws the band of frequencies, and shows the 1/√N reference.
The Beginner behavior must never depend on the level. When the learner drops to Beginner with an Intermediate source or basis, reset the lab and say so.
Make the interaction keyboard operable, with no pointer needed. Give every bar a text percentage and count. Announce each run, each stepper step, and each challenge result in an aria-live region, for example “100 simulated shots: outcome 0 appeared 47 times (47%); outcome 1 appeared 53 times (53%)”. Give the circuit a text description. Build the stepper as labelled regions, not as an animation that must be watched.
Do not add hardware, noise or readout error, SDK output, a third wire, mid-circuit measurement, Bloch spheres, or the CHSH game here.
Include three checks with immediate explanatory feedback that explains every wrong choice:
	A conceptual question: a qubit is measured and gives 1, then measured again right away. What does the second measurement give? The answer is 1. The feedback explains that the state now matches the first outcome, and explains why a fresh 50% draw is wrong.
	A prediction task: a qubit is in the mostly-0 state and is measured 100 times. Which counts are most likely? The options include 75/25, 50/50, and 100/0. The feedback links to the amplitudes squared and says the counts will not be exactly 75/25.
	A transfer task: tell |+⟩ from |−⟩ using the lab (round 2 of the Mystery state). The feedback explains the H and what changed.

5. Phase Shows Up After an H
Make the distinction explicit.
A direct Z-basis measurement
|+⟩ and |−⟩ both give 50% and 50%, so it cannot tell them apart.
A Z-basis measurement after an H
H turns |+⟩ into a certain 0 and |−⟩ into a certain 1.
Use the lab to show that:
A phase difference is invisible to a direct Z-basis measurement, and an H turns it into a probability difference.
Connect this to 02.04's “Z and the hidden phase”, which only looked at probabilities. Say that interference is the reason: the two paths through H add for |+⟩ and cancel for |−⟩.
Ask the learner to predict the result before trying it.
Say that two different states can give the same counts in one measurement and different counts in another.
Do not imply that the same counts mean the same state.

6. A Pair of Measurements
Introduce the Bell pair measured together, using the lab's pair mode.
Show that:
	The joint counts appear only at 00 and 11.
	Each qubit alone looks like a fair coin.
	Measuring q₀ first or q₁ first makes the second result certain, equal to the first.
	The joint statistics are the same whichever qubit goes first.
State three things and no more: the agreement is in the pair of results and appears only when the two lists are compared; neither measurement sends anything to the other, so the result at one end is a fair coin whatever happens at the other; the order of the two measurements does not change the statistics.
Do not say how the correlation arises, do not claim that the results were decided in advance, and do not claim that one measurement causes the other.
State the convention that q₀ is the left digit, and that other tools may print the bits the other way round. The outcomes 00 and 11 are unaffected.
Do not repeat the CHSH game.

Important Pedagogical Constraints
Do not imply:
A SHOT COUNT = A PROBABILITY
Instead teach:
A frequency describes one finite run. A probability describes the state.
Do not imply:
ONE MEASUREMENT REVEALS THE STATE
Instead teach:
One shot gives one result and cannot reveal the amplitudes.
Do not imply:
A MEASUREMENT = A GATE
Instead teach:
A gate moves a state and can be undone. A measurement gives a result and cannot be undone.
Do not imply that the state update is a physical mechanism. It is the rule of the ideal textbook model, stated as such.
Do not imply that the same counts mean the same state. |+⟩ and |−⟩ differ even though a direct measurement cannot tell them apart.
Do not imply that entangled qubits send signals, or that the Bell results were decided in advance. State only that the agreement shows when the results are compared and that no message passes.
Do not imply that the simulation is a device. Every run is ideal and noiseless, and real readout and noise are in Modules 04 and 05.
Do not imply that the bit order printed by this module is the one every tool uses.
Do not teach SDK code, results formats, real readout or noise, syndrome measurement, general measurement bases beyond naming them, mid-circuit measurement as a technique, three or more qubits, or the CHSH game here.
Keep Beginner V1 focused on the mental model required to understand why circuits need to be written as code and run.
