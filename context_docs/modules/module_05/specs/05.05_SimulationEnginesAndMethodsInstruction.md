This section continues Module 05 — Quantum Software Stack. 05.04 ended by asking how a simulator actually works out the answer, and why different simulators behave differently. This page answers that. It is a conceptual page, not an SDK run tutorial.
________________________________________
Goal
Teach the learner that a classical simulator is a program that models a quantum computation, that there are several methods for doing so which keep track of different things, and that each method's strengths and limits come from what it keeps, so no single method suits every circuit and no single number says how many qubits a simulator can handle.
The central progression is:
A SIMULATOR IS A TARGET THAT MODELS THE COMPUTATION → THE OBVIOUS METHOD KEEPS EVERY AMPLITUDE AND DOUBLES WITH EACH QUBIT → OTHER METHODS KEEP LESS BY USING STRUCTURE → EACH METHOD HANDLES SOME CIRCUITS AND NOT OTHERS → CHOOSING A SIMULATOR IS CHOOSING A TRADEOFF
The learner should understand that a simulator is a family of methods, not one thing.
The key conceptual transition is:
“A simulator is not one thing. It is a family of methods, each keeping track of the quantum state in a different way, and the way it keeps track decides which circuits it can handle and what it costs.”
This section should prepare the learner for Quantum Runtime and Classical-Quantum Orchestration (05.06).

Learning Outcomes
By the end of the section, the learner should be able to:
	Explain that a classical simulator is a program that models a quantum computation, and that it is one kind of target.
	Describe the state-vector method: it keeps every amplitude, so what it keeps doubles with each qubit.
	Explain that the 2ⁿ growth belongs to that one method, not to classical simulation as a whole.
	Describe, conceptually, what the stabilizer method keeps (a short list of rules the state obeys) and that it handles only circuits built from a restricted set of operations.
	Describe, conceptually, what the tensor-network method keeps (small pieces joined by links) and that it is cheap when entanglement is low and grows as entanglement grows.
	Recognize the same small state (the Bell state) as four amplitudes, as two rules, and as two pieces joined by a link.
	Say, for a given circuit and method, whether the method can handle it and what its size is doing as the circuit grows.
	Explain that no method is best for every circuit, and that “how many qubits can a simulator handle?” depends on the method and on the circuit.
	Distinguish what a simulator can show (the whole state, exact probabilities) from what a device returns (samples), and say that a simulator by default models an ideal device.
	Explain that the size of what a method keeps is not the same as how fast it runs, and that exact and approximate answers both exist.
	Distinguish a simulator's answer from a prediction of what a particular device will do.
	Ask what keeps track of the work of running a circuit, whichever target runs it.

Page Structure
1. From Targets to Simulators
Begin directly from the Hardware Abstraction and Backend Capabilities material.
Revisit the familiar progression:
A DEVICE REPORTS TIME-STAMPED ESTIMATES
↓
A SIMULATOR HAS NO PHYSICAL CONDITION TO REPORT
↓
BUT IT IS STILL A TARGET SOMETHING MUST COMPUTE ON
Then ask:
“If a simulator is just another kind of target, how does it actually work out the answer, and why do different simulators behave differently?”
Answer in one sentence: a simulator is a classical program that models the computation, using one of several methods, and the method decides what it can do.
Bring back the Bell circuit, drawn through QedCircuit, as the recurring example.
The learner should see a simulator as a family of methods rather than as one program.
Do not repeat the 05.01 explorer or the 05.04 explorer.

2. The Obvious Method: Keep Every Amplitude
Introduce the state-vector method.
Show the Bell state as the four amplitudes the method keeps. Produce them with the state-vector engine, not by typing them in.
Then add qubits. Show that what the method keeps is 2ⁿ amplitudes, doubling each time, and state the Module 01 assumption: 16 bytes per amplitude.
Connect to two earlier statements:
	Module 01 showed that storing the state grows quickly.
	05.04 said a simulator's limits are set by the computer that runs it. This is what that sentence means for this method.
Say that the wall belongs to this method.
Include a short explanation such as:
“Is this how all simulators work?”
“No. It is the obvious method. Others keep less by using structure.”
Do not imply that classical simulation as a whole needs 2ⁿ.

3. Keeping Less by Using Structure
Show the same Bell state in two more ways, each produced by its engine:
	Two rules the state obeys — the stabilizer method.
	Two small pieces joined by one link of size 2 — the tensor-network method.
Give one short paragraph each on what the method keeps.
Show the three forms side by side, labelled, with the point that they describe the same state.
The learner should understand:
ONE STATE
↓
THREE WAYS TO KEEP IT
↓
EACH KEEPS DIFFERENT THINGS
Do not imply that the three descriptions are interchangeable for every circuit.
Do not teach how the stabilizer table or a tensor network is updated.

4. What Each Method Is Good For, and Where It Stops
Compare the three methods in a compact table or three short blocks, using the same four questions:
	What it keeps.
	What circuits it handles.
	What drives its cost.
	What it gives back.
State-vector method
Keeps every amplitude. Handles any circuit. Cost is driven by the number of qubits. Gives back the whole state.
Stabilizer method
Keeps a short list of rules. Handles only circuits built from a restricted set of operations. Cost is driven by the number of qubits, and it grows slowly. Gives back outcome information for what it handles.
Tensor-network method
Keeps small pieces joined by links. Handles many circuits. Cost is driven by how entangled the state is. Gives back results as long as its pieces stay manageable.
Name once that other methods exist, for example methods that keep more than a state so that noise can be included.
Do not rank the methods, and do not describe any method as what real simulators always use.

5. Main Interactive — “Simulation Method Explorer”
Make this the primary V1 interaction.
Allow the learner to choose a circuit family, a number of qubits, and a method.
At minimum support:
	Choosing a circuit family: GHZ chain, GHZ chain with a T on every qubit, or Entangling layers. Give each a one-line description. The Bell circuit is the GHZ chain at two qubits.
	Choosing a number of qubits from 2 to 60. Mark 10 on the control: up to here the size is computed by running the method, and beyond it the size is estimated from the method's size formula.
	Choosing a depth, for Entangling layers only.
	Choosing a method: State vector, Stabilizer, or Tensor network. Show all three side by side; the chosen one is marked and drives the explanation.
	Resetting the view to its starting state.
When the learner chooses a family, a size, a depth, or a method:
	Show the circuit in words, and draw it through QedCircuit when it has two qubits. State whether every operation is in the stabilizer method's set.
	Show, for each method, what it keeps, its size with the tag “computed” or “estimated”, and whether it can handle this circuit. When it cannot, give the reason and name the first operation it cannot handle.
	Show what the size is doing, in a plain sentence per method, for example “doubles with each qubit”, “grows slowly”, or “link size has grown to …”.
	Show a small picture of what the chosen method keeps: an amplitude table for up to three qubits, a list of rules, or a chain of pieces with its link sizes.
Keep what the learner chooses (family, size, depth, method) separate from what is derived (what each method keeps and whether it can handle the circuit) and from what is observed (which method stays small, which stops, which grows).
The learner should visually experience:
CHOOSE A CIRCUIT, A SIZE, AND A METHOD
↓
SEE WHAT EACH METHOD MUST KEEP, AND WHETHER IT CAN
↓
NOTICE THAT NO METHOD WINS EVERYWHERE
Reinforce the distinction established in 05.04:
A simulator is a target with a description. How it computes is a separate matter, and that is the method.
Make the interaction resettable and keyboard accessible. Give every picture a text description. Present the size comparison as a real table, with stacked rows on a phone. Announce the verdict in an aria-live region when a choice changes. Keep focus where it was after the view re-renders.
Include these checks:
	Prediction task: “Adding one qubit to a state-vector simulation of the GHZ chain: what happens to what the method keeps?” The answer is that it doubles. Then compare with the explorer.
	Conceptual question: “A simulator handles a 40-qubit circuit easily. Does that mean it can handle every 40-qubit circuit?” Give feedback on each wrong choice: it depends on the method and on the circuit.
	Transfer task: “Which method cannot handle the GHZ chain with a T on every qubit, and why? Which can?” The stabilizer method cannot, because T is outside its restricted set. The state-vector and tensor-network methods can.
Do not add an SDK, a named simulator, a timing, noise parameters, a claim about advantage, or job submission.

6. What a Simulator Can Show That a Device Cannot
Make the distinction explicit.
A simulator
Can show the whole state (the Bell state's four amplitudes) and exact outcome probabilities. It can also produce samples, one outcome per shot, the way a device does.
A device
Returns only samples, one outcome per shot.
Say that a simulator by default models an ideal device. Say once that simulating noise needs more than a state vector and costs more, and tie this to the sentence on the previous page that a simulator can be set up to imitate a device's reported properties.
Say that a simulator's answer is not a prediction of what a particular device will do.
Use the Bell state to show that:
The same circuit gives the same ideal probabilities on a simulator, and a device returns samples from them, with whatever its own condition adds.
Do not teach noise models or their parameters.

7. No Method Wins Everywhere
Make the lesson of the explorer explicit.
Different circuits favour different methods:
	The GHZ chain is cheap for the stabilizer and tensor-network methods and doubles for the state-vector method.
	Adding a T stops the stabilizer method and leaves the tensor-network method cheap.
	Adding entangling layers makes the tensor-network method's pieces grow.
Say that “how many qubits can a simulator handle?” has no single answer, because it depends on the method and on the circuit.
Say that size is not speed: how long a simulation takes depends on the implementation, the hardware, and more, and this page gives no timings.
Add one hedged paragraph on exact versus approximate answers. The state-vector and stabilizer methods are exact for what they handle, up to rounding. The tensor-network method is exact when every link keeps its full size. Real tools may cap the link size to save memory, which makes the answer approximate. Do not demonstrate truncation.
Pick up the caveat from Module 01: structure is what the other two methods exploit. Make no claim about which circuits are hard or about quantum advantage.
Ask:
“A simulator handles a circuit easily. What does that tell you about the next one?”
Do not imply that structure makes simulation easy in general.

8. A Simulator Is a Target, Not a Forecast
Make the distinction explicit, in a short contrast block.
Simulator target
A target with a description (05.04) and a method (this page).
Device target
A target with a description and, usually, reported calibration data.
Prediction
What one target would do for one circuit. Neither a simulator target nor a device target is a forecast of the other.
Use the Bell circuit to show that:
The same circuit can run on both targets, and the results are two different kinds of thing.
Do not imply that a simulator result tells the learner what a device will return.

9. From Simulators to Runs
End by showing the simulator and the device as two targets, each of which will be handed work.
Previously:
A SIMULATOR MODELS THE COMPUTATION
↓
ITS METHOD DECIDES WHAT IT CAN DO
versus:
EITHER TARGET WILL BE HANDED WORK
↓
SOMETHING HAS TO COORDINATE THE RUN
Ask:
“Whether a circuit runs on a simulator or on a device, what sends the work, waits for it, and brings the results back?”
Then introduce the next problem: something has to coordinate the run.
Do not explain job lifecycles or orchestration here.
End with:
“Whether a circuit runs on a simulator or on a device, what sends the work, waits for it, and brings the results back?”

Technical Requirements
	Review and reuse existing QedGates, QedCircuit, QedNumbers, QedQuiz, QedSoftwareStack, button, callout, and reset components where appropriate.
	Represent each circuit family as data, an operation list for n qubits, and generate the descriptions, the engines' inputs, and the size comparison from that data rather than hardcoding a result per family.
	Define three circuit families, in the order 00, 01, 10, 11 with q0 as the left digit, as in Module 02:
	GHZ chain — H on the first qubit, then a CNOT from each qubit to the next. At two qubits this is the Bell circuit.
	GHZ chain with a T on every qubit — the same, followed by a T on every qubit. T is not in the stabilizer method's set.
	Entangling layers — for each layer, H and T on every qubit, then CNOTs between neighbouring qubits, alternating between the even and odd neighbouring pairs from layer to layer. The family takes a depth. All operations are deterministic, with no random numbers.
	Build the three engines for small, bounded use, not as production simulators:
	The state-vector engine runs up to 10 qubits (1,024 amplitudes).
	The stabilizer-table engine handles H, S, X, Z, and CNOT, and refuses any other operation, naming the first one it meets.
	Entanglement for the tensor-network method is computed from the real state vector across each cut along the line, up to 10 qubits, and the largest value is used as the link size. Do not implement a tensor-network engine.
	Beyond 10 qubits, use the size formulas and tag the result “estimated”. State the bounds used: for the GHZ chain and the GHZ chain with a T, the link size is 2; for Entangling layers, the link size is at most the smaller of 2 raised to the depth and 2 raised to half the number of qubits (each neighbouring CNOT can at most double the number of pieces across a cut, and at most one crosses the middle cut in a layer). Everything labelled “computed” must have been produced by running the engine at that size.
	Use these size formulas, and state them on the page: the state-vector method keeps 2ⁿ amplitudes (and, with 16 bytes per amplitude, 2ⁿ × 16 bytes); the stabilizer method keeps a table of about (2n)(2n + 1) bits; the tensor-network method, as a chain of pieces, keeps about n × 2 × χ² numbers, where χ is the largest link size. Say plainly that these are sizes, not speeds.
	Never show or compute a time, a speed, a benchmark, or a claim about a named simulator.
	Run the qubit control from 2 to 60, as on Module 01, and make the tags “computed” (up to 10) and “estimated” (beyond) visible everywhere a size is shown.

Important Pedagogical Constraints
Do not imply:
SIMULATING QUANTUM = ALWAYS 2ⁿ
Instead teach:
The 2ⁿ growth belongs to one method. Others keep less by using structure.
Do not imply:
A METHOD'S SIZE = ITS SPEED
Instead teach:
Size is how much a method keeps. How long it takes depends on much more, and this page gives no timings.
Do not imply:
ONE SIMULATOR HANDLES EVERY CIRCUIT OF A GIVEN SIZE
Instead teach:
What a simulator can handle depends on the method and on the circuit.
Do not imply:
A SIMULATOR'S ANSWER = WHAT A DEVICE WILL DO
Instead teach:
A simulator by default models an ideal device. Its answer is not a prediction of a particular device.
Do not imply:
STRUCTURE MAKES SIMULATION EASY
Instead teach:
Structure makes simulation cheap for circuits that have that structure.
Do not imply that classical simulation is impossible beyond some number of qubits, or the reverse. The page does not say which circuits are hard.
Do not imply that a quantum computer does or does not beat any simulation method. Make no claim about quantum advantage.
Do not imply that the size formulas are benchmarks, or that “computed” sizes describe any real simulator.
Do not imply that the three methods are the only methods, or that real simulators use exactly these descriptions.
Do not imply that a simulator can show the whole state and a device can do the same.
Do not teach SDK use, named simulators, timings or benchmarks, noise models, GPU or distributed simulation, algorithm design, or job submission here.
Keep Beginner V1 focused on the mental model required to understand why different simulators differ, and why running a circuit still needs something to coordinate it.
