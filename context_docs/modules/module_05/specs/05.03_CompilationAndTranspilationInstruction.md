## Open questions for review

Each has a recommendation. Say which you accept or change; the rest of the draft already assumes the recommendations.

1. **Which constraints at Beginner?** The taxonomy asks for native-operation and connectivity constraints "as motivations". *Recommendation:* two kinds, both shown on the two-qubit Bell circuit: **which operations a target allows** (its native operations) and **which way a two-qubit operation may point** (a connectivity constraint: a target may allow a CNOT from q₁ to q₀ and not the other way). Larger devices, where not every pair of qubits is connected, are **named in one sentence only**: showing routing needs three or more qubits, and `QedTwoQubit` and `QedTrace` are two-qubit modules. Routing is Intermediate or Advanced.

2. **Which targets?** *Recommendation:* three small **synthetic** targets, labelled as invented for the page and not real devices or vendors: **Open** (allows H, X, Z, and a CNOT either way: nothing to adapt), **One-way CNOT** (allows H, X, Z, and a CNOT only from q₁ to q₀), and **Rotations** (allows turns about the x and z axes, and a CNOT from q₀ to q₁, but no H). Each is data: a name, the operations it allows, and which two-qubit directions it allows.

3. **Which rewrites?** *Recommendation:* exactly three, each checked against the state simulator:
   - **H as three turns** (z by 90°, x by 90°, z by 90°), for a target with no H. Equal to H up to a global phase.
   - **Turning a CNOT round**: a CNOT from q₀ to q₁ is H on both qubits, a CNOT from q₁ to q₀, then H on both again. Exact.
   - **Cancelling a pair that undoes itself** (two H in a row on one qubit, for example). Exact.
   I checked these on the Bell circuit with the existing `QedTrace`: all three give the Bell state. On the One-way CNOT target, the Bell circuit goes from 2 operations to 6 after adapting and back down to 4 after cancelling, with one two-qubit operation throughout. That is a real tradeoff, not a manufactured one, so **the Bell circuit is enough** and no second example is needed. (Taxonomy section 6 allows one if Bell is too simple.)

4. **Where does the code live?** *Recommendation:* a **new pure module `QedTransform` (`transform.js`)**: the targets as data, `adapt(circuit, target)` (returns the new circuit and the steps taken, in words), `optimize(circuit)` (the cancelling rule and the steps), and `cost(circuit)` (operations and two-qubit operations; measurements are counted separately and never change). It depends on nothing and works on the `{ qubits, ops }` shape of `QedCircuit`. The **equivalence check goes in `QedTrace`** as `sameState(a, b)` (the same final state up to a global phase), since `QedTrace` is already the one module that composes the others. `QedTransform`'s rules are proven in tests with `loadModules`, against `QedTrace` and `QedGates`. The before and after circuits reuse `QedProgramForms` (instruction list, op ids) and `QedCircuit` (the diagram).

5. **How do we show that a transformation keeps the meaning?** *Recommendation:* at Beginner, show **the name of the state and the joint outcome bars before and after** (as on 02.06: "the Bell state; 00 and 11, half each"). Say plainly that real compilers rely on rules known to be valid, and that this check is a demonstration. **Amplitudes equal up to a global phase** are Intermediate. Do not use `QedProgramForms.sameComputation` here: it compares the operations written on each qubit, so it would call any transformed circuit different.

6. **How far does optimization go at Beginner?** *Recommendation:* one rule (cancel a pair that undoes itself), one idea (a rewrite can make a circuit shorter without changing what it does), and one caution (fewer operations is not the only goal: fewer two-qubit operations, a shorter circuit, or lower cost on a particular device can pull in different directions, and which matters depends on the target). Count **operations and two-qubit operations** only. Depth, scheduling, and any optimization algorithm are Intermediate or Advanced.

7. **What Intermediate and Advanced add.** *Recommendation:* as on 05.02, **V1 is Beginner-only**, with no gated blocks yet. Intermediate (future): the stages of a compiler as passes (decomposition, mapping, routing, scheduling), basis translation, amplitude-level equivalence checking, circuit depth. Advanced (future): routing and scheduling algorithms, optimization complexity, approximate transformations. Nothing above Beginner is tagged until it is built.

8. **Terminology.** Entries already exist for **Compilation, Transpilation, Quantum Compiler, Native Gate, Native Gate Set, Circuit Mapping, Qubit Mapping, Routing, Circuit Optimization, SWAP Gate, Connectivity, and Coupling**. *Recommendation:* reuse them and add this page to the references of the ones it substantively teaches (Compilation, Transpilation, Native Gate Set, Circuit Optimization). Add **one** new entry, **Gate Decomposition** (rewriting a gate as a sequence of other gates), and keep "adapting", "valid transformation", and "a target that allows…" as page-local wording. Do the full terminology check at build time, and record it in `llm/FLAGS.md`.

9. **Compilation versus transpilation.** The existing entries already say the words are used differently by different tools. *Recommendation:* the page says **compilation** is the responsibility of turning a program into something a target can run, and **transpilation** is a circuit-to-circuit rewrite that keeps its meaning, and it says that tools differ in how they use the two words. It does not assert how any SDK uses them.

10. **Where do the targets come from?** Native operations and directions are what 05.04 owns. *Recommendation:* here a target is a **small given description** ("this target allows…"), with no format shown and no field names, units, or numbers. The page ends by asking where such a description comes from and whether it can change, which is 05.04's opening. Do not claim that any operation is noisier or costlier on a device; say only that on a real device some operations may cost more than others, and that the next page says how software learns it.

11. **How is the comparison across targets shown?** The taxonomy's interaction is a "circuit transformation explorer comparing target-dependent implementations". *Recommendation:* the explorer shows **one target at a time**, and a **table computed from the same model** sets all three targets side by side (operations before, after adapting, after cancelling; two-qubit operations). The table is derived, never typed in.

12. **A teaser for 05.04.** *Recommendation:* close by asking "where does a target's list of what it allows come from, and could it change?" with one sentence about devices being tuned over time, and no calibration terms (05.04 owns them). Next: **Hardware Abstraction and Backend Capabilities →**

## Goal

Teach the learner that software transforms a program so a particular target can run it, that a valid transformation changes the form and not the meaning, and that adapting a program has a cost that software can sometimes reduce.

Central progression:

`A FORM SAYS WHAT TO COMPUTE → A TARGET ALLOWS ONLY SOME OPERATIONS → SOFTWARE REWRITES THE PROGRAM FOR THE TARGET → THE MEANING IS KEPT → ADAPTING HAS A COST, AND SOMETIMES IT CAN BE REDUCED`

Key conceptual transition: **"A program as written may not be a program a target can run. Software rewrites it into a form the target allows, keeping what it computes, and that rewrite can cost extra operations."**

It opens by answering the question 05.02 ended on ("if a form says what to compute, why can't a device just run it as written?") and ends by asking the question that motivates 05.04 ("where does a target's list of what it allows come from, and could it change?").

## Learning outcomes (Beginner)

The learner should be able to:

1. Explain that a program may need to be transformed before a particular target can run it.
2. Distinguish compilation (turning a program into something a target can run) from transpilation (a circuit-to-circuit rewrite), and say that tools use these words differently.
3. Give two kinds of limit a target can have: the operations it allows, and which way a two-qubit operation may point.
4. Explain that the limits belong to the target, so the same program may need different rewrites for different targets.
5. Describe a valid transformation as one that changes the form and keeps the meaning.
6. Recognize, in the explorer, a gate rewritten as a sequence of other gates, and a CNOT turned round.
7. Explain that adapting a program can add operations, and compare how many operations each target needs.
8. Explain that a rewrite can sometimes shorten a circuit without changing what it does, by cancelling a pair that undoes itself.
9. Explain that fewer operations is not the only goal, and that what is best depends on the target.
10. Distinguish compilation from running a program, from describing a target, and from runtime coordination.
11. Ask where a target's description of what it allows comes from, and whether it can change.

## Experience-level scope

**Beginner (V1) teaches:** the idea of transformation; compilation versus transpilation in plain words; two kinds of target limit shown on the Bell circuit; three synthetic targets; three rewrites; a valid transformation keeping the meaning (the state's name and outcomes, before and after); the cost of adapting and one way to reduce it; the tradeoff caution.

**Intermediate (future):** compiler passes (decomposition, mapping, routing, scheduling); basis translation; routing on a device where not every pair of qubits is connected; equivalence checked at the amplitude level; circuit depth.

**Advanced (future):** routing and scheduling algorithms; optimization complexity and heuristics; approximate transformations; compiler and runtime integration.

Do not build Intermediate or Advanced content to make V1 more complete.

**Not on this page (any level):** SDK setup or calls, real vendor target descriptions, backend property formats, calibration data or any numeric device properties (05.04), pulse or control representations, noise or error rates, simulation methods (05.05), job submission (05.06).

## Page structure (draft)

1. **From Forms to Transformation.** Reconnect to 05.02: the Bell computation in its forms, and its closing question ("why can't a device just run it as written?"). Name the answer in one sentence: because a target allows only some operations. Introduce the Bell circuit again as the recurring example.

2. **Same Meaning, Different Form.** The idea of a transformation: the program is rewritten, and what it computes stays the same. Show one small rewrite as a before and an after, with the state's name unchanged. Introduce **compilation** and **transpilation** in one sentence each, and say that tools use the words in different ways.

3. **Why a Target Cannot Always Run It As Written.** Two kinds of limit, one after the other, each shown on the Bell circuit and each a limit of a target and not of the program: *the operations it allows* (a target with no H), and *which way a two-qubit operation may point* (a target that allows a CNOT only one way). One sentence that bigger devices do not connect every pair of qubits, with nothing more. Say that each target is a small given description, and that where it comes from is the next page.

4. **Main Interactive: "Transformation Explorer."** The learner chooses a target. The page shows the original circuit, the adapted circuit (as a diagram and an instruction list), the steps taken in words, and the counts. A toggle cancels pairs that undo themselves. A check panel shows the state's name and the outcome bars before and after. Resettable and keyboard accessible. See below.

5. **What Adapting Costs.** The counts for each target, from the same model: operations before, after adapting, after cancelling, and two-qubit operations, in a computed table. The Open target needs nothing; the One-way CNOT target goes from 2 to 6 and back to 4; the Rotations target goes from 2 to 4. Say that adapting costs operations, and that cancelling pairs can win some back.

6. **A Valid Transformation Keeps the Meaning.** Revisit the check. A valid rewrite changes the form and not what the program computes, and real tools depend on rewrite rules known to be valid. Say that the check on this page is a demonstration. Contrast it with changing the computation. Name that some tools also make approximate changes, in one sentence and with no detail.

7. **Fewer Operations Is Not the Only Goal.** Short block on the tradeoff: fewer operations, fewer two-qubit operations, and a shorter circuit are different goals; which one matters depends on the target; trying harder to optimize also takes time. Do not claim that any operation is noisier on a real device.

8. **Compilation Is Not Execution, a Backend, or a Runtime.** Contrast block using the 05.01 responsibilities: compilation rewrites the program; a backend or target description says what a target allows; a runtime coordinates a run; execution carries it out. These are not interchangeable words.

9. **From Transformation to Targets.** Bridge. Show the three targets once more and ask: "Where does a target's list of what it allows come from, and could it change?" Introduce the next problem: software needs a description of each target, and a device is tuned over time. Do not teach target descriptions or calibration here. End with the question and `Next: Hardware Abstraction and Backend Capabilities →`.

(The skill's default arc has a "key distinction" subsection; here subsection 8 fills that role. The page has nine subsections because the cost and the tradeoff are separate ideas.)

## Main Interactive: "Transformation Explorer"

- **Controls:**
  - a choice of target: Open, One-way CNOT, Rotations (each with a one-line description of what it allows);
  - a toggle, "Cancel pairs that undo themselves";
  - reset.
- **Display, kept in separate places:**
  - the **target**: its name, the operations it allows, and which two-qubit directions it allows, labelled "invented for this page";
  - the **original circuit** and the **adapted circuit**, each drawn through `QedCircuit` and listed through `QedProgramForms`;
  - the **steps taken**, in words ("Replaced CNOT q₀→q₁ with H, H, CNOT q₁→q₀, H, H, because this target only allows the CNOT from q₁ to q₀");
  - the **counts**: operations and two-qubit operations before and after (measurements are the same in both and shown separately);
  - the **check**: the name of the state and the joint outcome bars, before and after.
- **Cause, response, observation:** the chosen target and the toggle are the cause; the adapted circuit and its steps are the response; the counts and the check are the observation.
- **Rules the page keeps:** a rewrite is applied only when the target needs it (the Open target changes nothing, and says so); every step names the limit that caused it; cancelling applies only to a pair on the same qubit with nothing between them.
- **Accessibility:** controls are keyboard-operable; each circuit has a text description and an instruction list; the steps and counts are text; an aria-live region announces a change of target and the check result.
- **Scope fence:** no real device, no SDK, no target format, no calibration data, no routing, no third qubit, no pulses.

### Checks

- *Prediction task:* "A target has no H. Before the explorer shows it, how many operations will the Bell circuit need there?" The learner picks from a few options, then compares with the explorer (4: three turns and the CNOT).
- *Conceptual question:* "A circuit is rewritten for a target. What must stay the same?" (what it computes) with feedback on each wrong choice, including that the number of operations may change and that the form always changes.
- *Transfer task:* turn the CNOT round for the One-way CNOT target by hand in the explorer's steps, and then say why cancelling saved two operations.

## Important pedagogical constraints (draft)

Do not imply:
- `COMPILATION = RUNNING THE PROGRAM`. Compilation rewrites a program; execution carries it out.
- `A TRANSFORMATION CHANGES WHAT THE PROGRAM COMPUTES`. A valid one changes only the form.
- `THERE IS ONE RIGHT COMPILED FORM`. Different targets, and different goals, give different forms.
- `FEWER OPERATIONS IS ALWAYS BETTER`, or `MORE OPERATIONS IS ALWAYS WORSE`. Which is best depends on the target and on the goal.
- `THE COMPILED CIRCUIT IS THE REAL PROGRAM`. It is another form of the same computation.
- `EVERY TARGET NEEDS THE SAME REWRITE`. The limits belong to the target.
- That the three targets are real devices, or that the limits shown are any vendor's.
- That compilation, backend description, and runtime are the same thing.
- That the check on this page is how real tools know a rewrite is valid.

Do not teach here: how a target's description is made or shaped, calibration or device properties, noise, routing or mapping algorithms, scheduling, depth, real SDK or compiler behavior, or any optimization algorithm.
