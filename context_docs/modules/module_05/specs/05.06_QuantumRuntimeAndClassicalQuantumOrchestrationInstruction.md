This section continues Module 05 — Quantum Software Stack. 05.05 ended by asking what sends the work, waits for it, and brings the results back. This page answers that. It is a conceptual page, not an SDK run tutorial.
________________________________________
Goal
Teach the learner that a quantum program does not run itself: a coordinating layer turns the adapted computation into a job, hands it to a target, keeps track of it, brings the results back in a form software can use, and lets the classical side decide what to run next, and that a loop of such jobs is how classical and quantum work are combined.
The central progression is:
AN ADAPTED PROGRAM → A JOB SENT TO A TARGET → TRACKED WHILE IT WAITS AND RUNS → RESULTS RETURNED AS COUNTS → CLASSICAL PROCESSING → THE NEXT JOB
The learner should understand that the runtime is the coordinator around a run, with four duties: send, track, return, and decide.
The key conceptual transition is:
“Running a circuit is not one step. A job is sent, tracked, and returned, and the classical side can use what comes back to decide what to send next.”
This section should prepare the learner for Integrated Quantum Software Workflows (05.07).
________________________________________
Learning Outcomes
By the end of the section, the learner should be able to:
	Explain that the runtime is the coordinator around a run, and name its four duties: send, track, return, and decide.
	Describe a job as a computation in a form a target can read, together with its settings, sent to a target and tracked.
	Describe the stages of a job’s life in plain words, and say that real platforms name and number them differently.
	Distinguish submitting a job from finishing it, and a finished job from a correct answer.
	Explain that a job can be rejected before it runs and can fail while running, and that a failure returns no results.
	Describe what comes back from a run: counts of outcomes over the shots, and say they are samples that differ from run to run.
	Describe result handling: counts become numbers the classical side can use, and those numbers are kept or acted on.
	Say what each side holds at each stage: the program side and the target.
	Describe a hybrid loop: results from one job help the classical side choose the next job.
	Say that every pass of a loop is a whole job, and what changes with fewer or more shots per job.
	Distinguish feedback between jobs from feedback inside a single run, and say that the second is handled close to the device.
	Distinguish the runtime from compilation, from a backend description, and from execution, and say that a status says nothing about how long a wait will be.
	Ask what changes and what stays the same when all the responsibilities work together on one computation.

Page Structure
1. From Targets to Runs
Begin directly from the Simulation Engines and Methods material.
Revisit the familiar progression:
A SIMULATOR IS A TARGET WITH A METHOD
↓
A DEVICE IS A TARGET WITH A DESCRIPTION AND REPORTED DATA
↓
EITHER TARGET WILL BE HANDED WORK
Then ask:
“Whether a circuit runs on a simulator or on a device, what sends the work, waits for it, and brings the results back?”
Answer in one sentence: a runtime sends the work, tracks it, brings the results back, and lets the classical side decide what comes next.
Name the four duties as four verbs: send, track, return, decide.
Bring back the Bell circuit, drawn through QedCircuit, as the recurring example.
The learner should see running a circuit as several coordinated steps rather than one.
Do not repeat the 05.01 stepper.

2. A Job: The Work in a Form a Target Can Read
Introduce the job.
A job is the adapted computation plus its settings, with a number so it can be tracked.
Clearly distinguish:
	Computation — the adapted program, as an ordered list of instructions (the form from 05.02, the result of 05.03).
	Settings — the target and the number of shots.
	Job number — how the program side refers to the job while it is tracked.
Show the Bell job’s payload through QedProgramForms.instructionListHTML.
Do not imply that a job is a picture of the circuit, or the compiled circuit alone.
Include a short explanation such as:
“Is a job the same as a circuit?”
“No. A circuit describes the computation. A job is the computation in a form a target can read, plus the settings and a number, sent to one target.”

3. A Job’s Life
Introduce the lifecycle as an illustrative model, labelled so on the page.
Prepared → Submitted → Queued → Running → Finished
Three exits:
	Rejected — the target refuses the job at submission.
	Failed — the run stops partway, with no results.
	Cancelled — the program withdraws the job.
Say that retrieving the results is a separate action after Finished, not a state.
Say that real platforms use different names and numbers of states, and may not queue at all. A simulator running on the same computer may start at once. A shared service may queue even for a simulator.
Say that a status such as “queued” tells you nothing about how long the wait will be.
Introduce the two lanes: the program side and the target.
Introduce the word “moment”: the page counts steps, never seconds.
The learner should understand:
SUBMITTED
↓
NOT YET RUN
↓
FINISHED MEANS THE RUN COMPLETED
Do not name a platform, a real status string, or a duration.

4. Main Interactive — “Run Lifecycle Explorer”
Make this the primary V1 interaction.
Allow the learner to send one Bell-circuit job and step through its life.
At minimum support:
	Choosing a target: Simulator or Device Q4, both the invented targets from 05.04.
	Choosing a number of shots: for example 10, 100, or 1000.
	Choosing an outcome: the job finishes; the target rejects it at submission; the run fails partway.
	Step, Back, Cancel (while the job is queued or running), Run it again, and Reset.
When the learner chooses a target, shots, or outcome, and steps through the job:
	Show the lanes: the program side and the target, each listing what it holds at this moment, with the item that just crossed between them marked.
	Show the status: the current state with its glyph and label, the moment number, and, on the device, the invented number of jobs ahead.
	Show the result, after Finished: the counts for each bit string, the shot total, the target’s name, and a plain sentence that these are samples and that running the job again gives different counts.
	After Rejected or Failed, show “no results” and the reason in words.
	Show the job’s payload as the instruction list, with any operation the target does not list marked by a text tag.
Define the outcomes this way:
	Finishes — on the device the job carries the Bell circuit adapted to Device Q4 (05.03); on the simulator it carries the Bell circuit as written. The device waits in the queue for a few moments; the simulator does not.
	Rejected — the job carries an operation the target’s description does not list. On Device Q4 it is the Bell circuit as first written (H is not listed). On the simulator it is the Bell circuit followed by a T gate (T is not listed). The target says which operation, and the page offers “Adapt the circuit and send it again” (device, using the adaptation from 05.03) or “Remove the T and send it again” (simulator). Either starts a new job with a new number.
	Fails partway — the job is accepted and runs, then stops. State that no results come back, with an invented reason, and that a failed job is not a wrong answer.
Keep what the learner chooses (target, shots, outcome, steps) separate from what is derived (the lanes and the status) and from what is observed (what comes back, or that nothing does).
The learner should visually experience:
CHOOSE A TARGET AND AN OUTCOME
↓
WATCH THE JOB MOVE BETWEEN THE TWO SIDES
↓
SEE WHAT COMES BACK, OR THAT NOTHING DOES
Reinforce the distinction established in 05.03 and 05.04:
Compilation rewrites the program. The description says what a target allows. The runtime coordinates the run.
Make the interaction resettable and keyboard accessible. Give the lifecycle a text description and an ordered-list alternative. Present the counts as a real table, with stacked rows on a phone. Announce each step’s status and what crossed in an aria-live region. Keep focus where it was after the view re-renders.
Include these checks:
	Prediction task: “You submit a job and its status is Queued. What does that tell you about how long the wait will be?” The answer is nothing. Then compare with the explorer.
	Conceptual question: “A job’s status is Finished. Does that mean the answer is correct?” Give feedback on each wrong choice: it means the run completed, and whether the results are useful depends on the program, the target, and how they are interpreted.
	Transfer task: “A job is Failed. What results come back? What would you do next?” None, which is different from results that are wrong; then decide whether to resubmit, change the job, or choose another target.
Do not add an SDK, polling code, a named platform, a timing, pricing, or a noise model.

5. Results: From Counts to Numbers
Show what comes back and what the classical side does with it.
Show three steps:
	The target returns counts: for each shot, one outcome, collected per bit string, with the shot total and the target’s name.
	The classical side turns counts into something it can use: a fraction, which is an estimate of a probability, and for the Bell circuit how often the two bits agreed.
	The classical side keeps or acts on it: prints it, stores it, or feeds the next job.
Make three points:
	Counts are samples, not the exact probabilities. A simulator can give exact ones (05.05), but the default result for both targets is counts.
	Running the same job again gives different counts, because each submission is a new job.
	A finished job is not a correct answer. A failed job returns nothing at all.
Counts are drawn with QedShots. Use a seed, so the first run is the same on every load and Run it again gives new counts. Label every count as simulated.
The simulator path shows ideal counts: only 00 and 11 for Bell. The device path shows the same pattern with a few other bit strings, a small fixed share set in the model and labelled invented, as 05.01 already said a device can return.
Do not teach noise models, and do not present the invented share as typical of any device.

6. When the Next Job Depends on the Last: Hybrid Loops
Extend the previous interaction from one job to a loop of jobs.
Use a small additional example, because Bell has nothing to vary between runs: a one-qubit rotation whose angle the classical side adjusts.
Introduce the term:
Hybrid loop
The learner should discover that a loop is the lifecycle repeated, with the classical side deciding the next job from the last results.
Show a loop panel that shares the model with the explorer:
	The program sets an angle θ and sends a job that applies the rotation and measures.
	The counts come back, and the program estimates the fraction of 1s.
	A scripted rule sets the next angle, until the estimate is near the stated target or the pass limit is reached.
Define the loop:
	Start at 30°. The stated target is a fraction of 1s of 0.75, which the ideal angle of 120° gives.
	The starting rule moves the angle by 100° times the gap between the target and the estimate, kept between 0° and 180°. Stop when the estimate is within 0.05 of the target, or after 10 passes. If the build shows that the rule does not settle with 500 shots inside the pass limit, adjust the constant and record it in llm/FLAGS.md.
	On the device path, a small fixed share of reported bits is flipped, a number set in the model and labelled invented. The loop steers by what comes back, so it settles at a slightly different angle.
Support:
	Choosing a target: Simulator or Device Q4.
	Choosing shots per job: for example 20, 100, or 500.
	Step, Run to the end, and Reset.
Show a real table with one row per pass: angle sent, shots, counts of 0 and 1, estimate, next angle. Stack the rows on a phone. Show running totals for jobs submitted and shots used, and the stated target and rule in words.
The learner should discover that every pass is a whole job, so a loop of ten passes is ten jobs. With few shots per job the estimate jumps and the angle wanders. With many it steadies and uses more of the target.
Ask:
“If each pass is a whole job, what does a loop of ten passes ask of the runtime?”
Say that the rule is one simple, scripted rule among many. Say that algorithms which use this loop shape are taught elsewhere in the curriculum, without a link.
Show totals of jobs and shots, never time.
Do not teach optimizers, gradients, cost functions, VQE, or any named algorithm. Do not claim that a loop is better than anything.

7. Feedback Between Jobs and Inside a Run
Make the distinction explicit, in a short contrast block.
Between jobs
The loop: results come back through the runtime, and the classical side sends the next job.
Inside a run
Some circuits measure a qubit partway through and use the result to choose a later operation in the same run (Mid-Circuit Measurement). That happens close to the device, in the control software (the boundary from 05.04), too fast to go through job submission.
Do not demonstrate feedback inside a run.
Say that real-time feedback, latency, and resource management are for later, deeper material.
Say that a hybrid loop does not put the quantum side in charge. The classical side sets the angle, sends the job, reads the results, and decides what happens next.
Do not imply that all feedback happens between jobs.

8. The Runtime Is Not the Compiler, the Backend, or the Run
Make the distinction explicit, extending 05.03.
Compilation
Rewrites the program so a target can run it.
Backend or target description
Says what a target allows. It decides rejection.
Runtime
Coordinates the run, from sending to returning.
Execution
Carries the run out.
Use the lifecycle to show where each acts: compilation before the job, the description when the target decides on rejection, the runtime throughout, and execution while the job runs.
Say that a platform may combine or reorder these, and that 05.07 compares complete workflows.
Do not imply that these words are interchangeable.

9. From Responsibilities to Workflows
End by comparing each responsibility on its own with all of them working on one computation.
Previously:
EACH RESPONSIBILITY ON ITS OWN
↓
ONE PAGE FOR EACH
versus:
ONE COMPUTATION
↓
ON A SIMULATOR AND ON A DEVICE
↓
ALL THE RESPONSIBILITIES AT WORK
Ask:
“You have met every responsibility one at a time. When all of them work on one computation, on a simulator and on a device, what stays the same, and what changes?”
Then introduce the next problem: put it all together.
Say that the order and grouping of the responsibilities differ between platforms.
Do not explain the full workflows here.
End with:
“You have met every responsibility one at a time. When all of them work on one computation, on a simulator and on a device, what stays the same, and what changes?”

Important Pedagogical Constraints
Do not imply:
SUBMITTED = FINISHED
Instead teach:
Submitting hands the work over. The run happens later.
Do not imply:
FINISHED = CORRECT
Instead teach:
Finished means the run completed. Whether the results are useful depends on the program, the target, and how they are interpreted.
Do not imply:
A STATUS = A TIME ESTIMATE
Instead teach:
“Queued” says nothing about how long a wait will be.
Do not imply:
A FAILED JOB = A WRONG ANSWER
Instead teach:
A failed job returns nothing.
Do not imply:
COUNTS = THE EXACT PROBABILITIES
Instead teach:
Counts are samples and differ from run to run.
Do not imply that the page’s states are the states of any real platform. They are an illustrative model; real platforms name and number them differently and may not queue.
Do not imply that the runtime, the compiler, the backend description, and the run are the same thing.
Do not imply that a hybrid loop puts the quantum side in charge. The classical side decides what is sent next.
Do not imply that a hybrid loop is an algorithm, or that a loop is better than anything. Make no claim about quantum advantage.
Do not imply that all feedback happens between jobs. Some happens inside a run, close to the device.
Do not imply that every platform has a queue, or that waiting dominates every loop.
Do not present the invented extra bit strings or the invented flip share as typical of any device.
Do not teach SDK use, polling code, named platforms, real statuses or timings, pricing, algorithm design or optimizers, error correction, calibration physics, or noise models here.
Keep Beginner V1 focused on the mental model required to understand why running a circuit takes coordination, and why the responsibilities must be put together.
