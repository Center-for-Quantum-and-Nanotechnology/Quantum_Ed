Goal
Teach the learner that a superconducting qubit is a tiny engineered electrical circuit, kept cold, controlled and read with microwaves, fast and short-lived, linked to fixed neighbours, and hard to scale because every qubit needs wiring into a refrigerator, and that this is one answer to the same six questions every platform has to answer.
The central progression is:
THE FIVE NEEDS, AGAIN → A CIRCUIT THAT ACTS LIKE AN ATOM → KEPT COLD → CONTROLLED AND READ WITH MICROWAVES → FAST AND SHORT-LIVED → FIXED NEIGHBOURS → WIRING AS A LIMIT → THE BELL CIRCUIT’S THREE OPERATIONS HERE → TO BE CONTINUED
The learner should understand that Hardware Fundamentals followed one invented qubit, and that a real platform answers the same questions with a real object, and that every answer has a cost.
The key conceptual transition is:
“Hardware Fundamentals followed one invented qubit. A real platform answers the same questions with a real object, and every answer has a cost.”
This section should prepare the learner for Trapped-Ion Qubits (04.02.02), by asking what a different physical system would do with the same six questions.

Learning Outcomes
By the end of the section, the learner should be able to:
	Say that a superconducting qubit is a tiny engineered electrical circuit that behaves like an atom with two usable levels.
	Say that no two such qubits come out exactly alike, and that this is why each is measured and tuned.
	Explain why the circuit is kept very cold, in two reasons, and say that cold is not universal.
	Say that the qubit is controlled by microwave signals sent down wires from room-temperature electronics.
	Say that it is read by a resonator whose response changes with the qubit’s state, and that the signal is weak.
	Say, in roughly stated terms, that its operations are fast and its qubits forget quickly, and read an operations-that-fit range as a budget and not a promise.
	Say that its qubits sit at fixed places and interact with linked neighbours, and that unlinked qubits need extra operations.
	Give three reasons it is hard to scale: wiring and cooling, fabrication variation, and a crowded chip.
	Say how the Bell circuit’s three operations are done on this platform.
	Say which of 04.01.05’s five sources of noise each part of the device brings, and that which one is largest depends on the device and the time.
	Read the Platform Card’s six questions, say that the figures on it are approximate ranges that change quickly, and find the sources behind them at the bottom of the page.
	Ask how a different physical system would answer the same questions.

	How is it controlled?

Say that every platform in this group fills in the same card, and that the page fills it in a row at a time.
The learner should see a real platform as a set of answers to questions already asked, rather than a new subject.

2. What Is the Qubit?
Introduce the qubit as an engineered artificial atom.
It is a small electrical circuit, built on a chip from a metal that becomes superconducting when it is cold, with one small element in it called a Josephson junction.
Say that the junction is what makes the circuit’s levels unevenly spaced, which is exactly the property 04.01.01 said a good qubit needs, so that a drive can single out its two lowest levels.
Reuse QedLadder to show the engineered ladder again as a reminder, with the same invented units, labelled invented.
Name the transmon once as the common design, in a sentence. Do not derive it.
Say that it is made by fabrication, so no two qubits come out exactly alike. Link back to 04.01.01 and to the “Why Qubits Differ” table of 04.01.05.
Say that the circuit’s frequency is in the microwave range, a few gigahertz [1].
Include a short explanation such as:
“Is it a real atom?”
“No. It is a circuit built to behave like one: two usable levels, spaced so a signal can pick them out.”
This answers Platform Card question 1.
Do not give equations, circuit theory, materials, or a derivation.

3. Why It Is Kept Cold
Give two reasons, plainly.
The metal has to be superconducting, which it only is when cold.
The qubit’s two levels are close in energy, so even a little heat would jostle it. This is 04.01.01’s gap against heat again.
Show the gap-against-heat comparison with QedLadder.startShares at the three invented temperatures of 04.01.01, as a small comparison, labelled invented.
Then state the real operating temperature as the approximate range, roughly ten to twenty millikelvin, a hundredth of a degree above absolute zero [2][3]. Say that the refrigerator that gets there is called a dilution refrigerator.
Show one computed line, tagged “computed from the stated ranges”: a qubit of a few gigahertz [1] has an energy equal to the heat at roughly a few tenths of a kelvin, so at ten to twenty millikelvin the heat is smaller than the qubit’s energy by a factor of several to tens. Generate it from the ranges in QedPlatform.
State that cold is not universal. Trapped ions and neutral atoms sit in vacuum and are handled with lasers, and the next pages show them.
The learner should understand:
A CIRCUIT THAT IS ONLY A QUBIT WHEN COLD
↓
A REFRIGERATOR AROUND IT
↓
COLD IS THIS PLATFORM’S ANSWER, NOT QUANTUM COMPUTING’S
Do not teach superconductivity. Do not give a thermal formula.

4. Main Interactive — “Chip and Fridge”
Make this the primary V1 interaction.
Allow the learner to choose one of six parts of a superconducting device and see its role.
At minimum support:
	Part: one of six, chosen by a real button and by selecting it in the picture: the qubit, the coupler, the control line, the readout resonator, the cold stages, and the wiring.
	Reset, which shows the picture whole with no part chosen.
The six parts are:
	The qubit — a small circuit with a junction in it.
	The coupler — the element that links a qubit to its neighbour.
	The control line — the wire that carries microwaves to a qubit.
	The readout resonator — a small circuit beside the qubit whose response changes with the qubit’s state, and the line that carries its signal.
	The cold stages — the stack of colder and colder plates in the refrigerator, with the shielding.
	The wiring — the many lines running from the room-temperature electronics down to the chip.
When the learner chooses a part:
	Show the schematic (shown): the chip with two linked qubits, their couplers, a control line and a readout resonator for each, the stack of cold stages with the chip at the bottom, and the wiring running down to room-temperature electronics, all labelled “simplified, not to scale”. It is a schematic and not a layout of any real chip.
	Show the part’s role (derived), in separate lines: what it is, what it does, which of the five needs it serves (with the number badge used on 04.01.01 and 04.01.05), which source of noise it brings (an orange tag, from 04.01.05’s five), and which Platform Card question it answers.
	Show the card row (observed): the Platform Card’s row for that question, highlighted, so the learner sees how the picture and the card relate. The row shows only what the card says.
The parts map to the needs, the sources, and the card as data:
	The qubit — needs: start and keep — sources: the environment and leakage — card question 1.
	The coupler — need: connect — source: the neighbours — card question 5.
	The control line — need: control — source: the control — card question 2.
	The readout resonator — need: read — source: the readout — card question 3.
	The cold stages — need: keep — source: the environment — card question 1.
	The wiring — needs: control and read — sources: the control and the environment — card question 6.
The learner should visually experience:
A PART OF THE DEVICE
↓
ITS ROLE: WHAT IT IS, WHAT IT DOES, THE NEED IT SERVES, THE SOURCE IT BRINGS
↓
THE CARD ROW IT FILLS
Reinforce the distinction established in the taxonomy:
Every part answers a question the card asks, and brings a cost the card records.
Make the interaction resettable and keyboard accessible. Give a text list of the six parts with the same facts as the picture. Give the picture a text description. Announce the part’s name, its need, and its source in an aria-live region when it changes. Keep focus where it was after the view re-renders. Present tables as real tables, with stacked rows on a phone.
Include these checks:
	Prediction task: “A superconducting chip has twice as many qubits. What happens to the number of lines running into the refrigerator?” The answer is that it grows with them, and that sharing lines slows the growth but does not stop it. Then compare with the wiring model in section 7.
	Conceptual question: “Is a superconducting qubit cold because the qubit is a cold object?” Give feedback on each wrong choice: no, the metal must be superconducting and the qubit’s levels are close in energy, so heat would jostle it, and other platforms are not cooled this way.
	Transfer task: “A platform has fast gates and short lifetimes. Is it a good platform?” The answer is that this is a trade-off, that how many operations fit is a budget and not a promise, and that the comparison is later. Then ask: “Where would you look for what goes wrong first?” The answer is the five sources of noise, and the device’s own measured figures.
Do not add a vendor, a real device, a qubit count, a ranking, or a derivation.

5. How Long, and How Fast
Show the card’s fourth row as three ranges, each tagged “approximate” and cited:
	One-qubit gate: roughly tens of nanoseconds [4].
	Two-qubit gate: roughly tens to a few hundred nanoseconds [1][2][4][5].
	Readout: roughly a fraction of a microsecond to about a microsecond [4][6].
	Energy-loss time (T1) and phase-loss time (T2): roughly tens to hundreds of microseconds, with the best laboratory devices over a millisecond [2][4][7].
Then show the computed operations that fit in a lifetime, as an order of magnitude: hundreds to a few thousand. Label it “computed from typical values in the sources; the ends of the ranges are not one device”. Say that it is a budget and not a promise, in the wording of 04.01.04 (“a long time is not a guarantee”).
This is the only computed comparison on the page.
Say that the figures change quickly, and that the sources are at the bottom of the page.
Ask:
“A platform’s qubits last a short time, and its gates are fast. Is that good or bad?”
Answer: it is a trade-off. Fast operations leave room for many of them inside a short memory. Whether that is an advantage depends on what else the platform can do, and the comparison between platforms is 04.02.06’s.
Keep the Quantum Computing Today promise: fast, and forgets quickly.
Do not rank. Do not say “better” or “worse”. Do not give one number.

6. How Qubits Connect
Show a generic pattern of nearest neighbours, with no vendor layout.
Each qubit sits at a fixed place on the chip, linked to a few neighbours through a coupler, which is a fixed element or one whose strength can be tuned. Name this and do not derive it.
A two-qubit gate is performed between neighbours that are linked. This is the coupling idea of 04.01.02 with its platform.
Two qubits that are not linked need extra operations to bring them together.
Name Module 05’s fence once: what software does about this (routing, the coupling map, native gates) is Module 05. Link to 05.04 once. Do not duplicate QedBackend’s field catalog.
This answers Platform Card question 5.
Do not teach routing. Do not draw any real device’s layout.

7. What Makes It Hard to Scale
Give three reasons, one line each, with a small picture or number.
Wiring and cooling — every qubit needs its own control line and some readout wiring from room temperature to the chip, and the refrigerator has limited room and limited cooling power [3]. Say that real processors use several control lines for every qubit, and that readout lines are shared among qubits [2][5].
Show the invented wiring model, labelled invented: choose a number of qubits, and switch the sharing of readout lines on or off. Show the number of lines, as bars with labelled rows. Say that the number of lines grows with the number of qubits, and that sharing helps but does not remove the growth.
Fabrication — no two qubits come out alike, so each must be measured and tuned (04.01.05’s loop), and more qubits mean more tuning and more chance of a bad one.
A crowded chip — more neighbours means more ways to disturb them (04.01.05’s neighbours).
Say that how any of this is being worked on is not a Beginner topic, and that the page does not predict a limit.
This answers Platform Card question 6.
Do not give a record qubit count. Do not predict how many qubits are possible.

8. The Bell Circuit, Here
Show the module’s recurring strip, “how would this platform do it?”, for the Bell circuit’s three kinds of operation. Reuse QedCircuit.
	A one-qubit rotation — a microwave signal on the qubit’s control line.
	A two-qubit interaction — a gate between linked neighbours, through the coupler.
	A readout — the resonator.
Then add one short section, what usually goes wrong here, tied to 04.01.05’s five sources, in words and without numbers:
	The environment — the cold stages and shielding are there to keep it out, and lifetimes are short.
	The control — microwave lines and electronics, and a setting that drifts.
	The neighbours — fixed neighbours disturb each other.
	Leakage — levels outside the qubit’s two.
	The readout — a weak signal.
Read the five source names and their plain words at run time from QedNoise.SOURCES.
Say plainly that which source is largest depends on the device and changes over time, as the Error Budget showed, and that this page ranks none.
This section is flagged for hardware review.
Do not give a ranking or a number for any source.

9. The Platform Card
Show the complete card: six numbered rows, each with its question, its answer in plain words, and a tag where a value is approximate or not applicable. Each approximate value carries its numbered citation.
	Row 1 — What is the qubit? A tiny engineered circuit on a chip, made from a superconducting metal and built around a Josephson junction, usually a transmon. Kept at roughly ten to twenty millikelvin in a dilution refrigerator.
	Row 2 — How is it controlled? Microwave signals along wires from room-temperature electronics.
	Row 3 — How is it read out? A resonator beside the qubit whose response changes with its state. A weak microwave probe is sent in and the changed reflection is amplified.
	Row 4 — How long does it last, and how fast does it run? The ranges of section 5, and the computed operations that fit.
	Row 5 — How do qubits connect? Fixed neighbours, linked through couplers.
	Row 6 — What makes it hard to scale? Wiring and cooling, fabrication variation, and a crowded chip.
Show the note “Figures change quickly. They are approximate ranges taken from the sources at the bottom of the page, last checked 2026-11-03, and no range describes one device.”

Important Pedagogical Constraints
Do not imply:
A SUPERCONDUCTING QUBIT = THE STANDARD QUBIT = THE BEST PLATFORM
Instead teach:
It is one answer to six questions, and its answers have costs.
Do not imply:
COLD = QUANTUM COMPUTING
Instead teach:
Being cold is a property of this platform. Other platforms use vacuum and lasers.
Do not imply:
FAST BUT FORGETS QUICKLY = A VERDICT
Instead teach:
It is a trade-off, and the comparison is 04.02.06’s.
Do not imply that a range is a device. The ends of a range are not one device, and every figure changes quickly.
Do not imply that the schematic is a real chip, or that the wiring model’s numbers are real.
Do not imply that unlinked qubits cannot interact. They need extra operations, and how software handles that is Module 05.
Do not imply that the sources of noise on this platform are ranked. Which is largest depends on the device and the time.
Do not imply that a figure on the card is settled. It is a range taken from the sources, last checked on a date, and awaiting a hardware expert’s review.
Do not name a vendor, a product, a record qubit count, or a single device figure.
Do not teach circuit quantization, pulse shaping, electronics, routing, coupling maps, error vocabulary, or any other platform here.
Keep Beginner V1 focused on the mental model required to understand why the answers depend on what the qubit is made of.

The learner should leave with:
ONE INVENTED QUBIT, THEN A REAL ONE
↓
A CIRCUIT, KEPT COLD, CONTROLLED AND READ WITH MICROWAVES
↓
FAST, SHORT-LIVED, WITH FIXED NEIGHBOURS AND A WIRE FOR EVERY QUBIT
↓
SIX ANSWERS, EACH WITH A COST
And understand the central relationship:
A circuit → cold → microwaves → fast and short-lived → fixed neighbours → wiring as a limit.
The central takeaway should be:
A superconducting qubit is an engineered circuit that answers the six questions with a refrigerator, microwaves, and wires. Each answer has a cost, the figures are approximate and change quickly, and it is one answer among several.
End with:
“What if the qubits remembered much longer, and any two could interact?”
