**Weight of this page:** it opens the group the taxonomy calls "the same questions, answered by different physical systems", and it sets the pattern for the next four pages and the comparison. Seven risks to watch:
- **The first platform fact in the module.** Every earlier page used invented numbers. This page must state **real, dated, contestable facts** (a temperature, gate times, lifetimes) and the taxonomy's rules apply in full: **ranges and orders of magnitude, labelled approximate, never a single value, never a ranking, no vendor or product names, a "figures change" note and a dated sources list, and a hardware review before they are treated as settled** (decisions 6 and 14). The ranges in Open question 3 are **proposed from general knowledge and have not been checked against sources**; the final spec must not be written until they are.
- **A brochure.** Without a fixed shape, five platform pages become five product descriptions. The **Platform Card** (the six questions of taxonomy section 9, in the same order) is the page's spine, and this page defines it for the module (Open question 2).
- **A physics course in disguise.** A superconducting circuit can be taught at graduate depth (the junction, the anharmonic oscillator, circuit quantization). The Beginner page **names** the junction and the transmon and **derives** nothing.
- **A sameness the module must not manufacture.** The Bell circuit's three operations are an anchor and not a restriction, and each platform has a different hard step.
- **"Cold" as universal.** The module's misconception "cold is not universal" starts here. This platform needs a refrigerator, and the next ones need vacuum and lasers.
- **Naming a winner by omission.** "Fast but forgets quickly" must read as a trade-off, and the page must not imply it is the better or the worse platform.
- **Neighbours.** Module 05 owns the connectivity map and native gates as software sees them, and 04.01.05 already told the learner the platform pages would say which source dominates. This page names each fence once (Open questions 8 and 10).

**Why a new shared model is needed:** the page needs a data-driven **Platform Card** (the six answers, each with its words, its approximate ranges, its "not applicable" cases, and its dated sources) that four more pages and the comparison reuse, plus the **parts of a chip and a fridge**, each mapped to the need it serves and the source of noise it brings, and a small **wiring model**. No built module has these. 04.02.02 to 04.02.05 each add a card to the same data, and 04.02.06 shows the cards side by side and computes the comparison from them.

## Open questions for review

Each has a recommendation. Say which you accept or change; the rest of the draft already assumes the recommendations.

1. **What is the one idea the page teaches?** The taxonomy row says "how an electrical circuit can act as a qubit and what that implies for control, speed, and scale". *Recommendation:* **a superconducting qubit is a tiny electrical circuit, built on a chip and cooled until it behaves like an atom with two usable levels. Because it is a circuit, it is controlled and read with microwaves sent down wires, it is very fast, it forgets quickly, its neighbours are fixed in place, and every qubit added needs its own wiring into a refrigerator.** Four supporting points, shown and not derived: it is **engineered** (no two are alike), it is **cold** (and cold is not universal), it is **fast and short-lived** (a trade-off, not a verdict), and **wiring and fabrication are what make it hard to scale**. Cross-platform comparison is 04.02.06's job, and this page does not rank.

2. **What is the Platform Card, and how does this page define it for the module?** Taxonomy section 9 fixes the six questions, and decision 11 asks for one data-driven component and one data set, with a "compare with" control. *Recommendation:* a **new shared module `QedPlatform` (`platform.js`)** holds the six **questions** (fixed text, fixed order: what is the qubit, how is it controlled, how is it read out, how long does it last and how fast does it run, how do qubits connect, what makes it hard to scale), the **data for each platform**, and the **card renderer**. Every answer has plain words, and a field that does not apply is shown as "not applicable" and never hidden (taxonomy section 9). **This page ships the schema and its own card only.** Each later page adds its own card to the same data, and the **"compare with" control appears only when a second card exists**, so no page offers a comparison with a platform that has not been written, reviewed, and built. *Why not author all four cards now:* four platforms of unreviewed facts would have to be reviewed before the first page can be trusted, and the other platforms' pages would be written around data written earlier. The draft therefore asks for the schema to be general enough for all four (including the cases that do not fit: a photon has no "chip temperature", and a dual-rail encoding is not a platform) and for each later page to extend it. 04.02.06 computes its comparison from the same data.

3. **Which figures does the page state, and how are they kept honest?** *Recommendation:* **only the figures the Platform Card needs, as approximate ranges, and each flagged for verification.** The ranges below are **my proposal from general knowledge, not checked against any source, and they must be verified (and reviewed by a hardware expert) before the final spec** (taxonomy decision 14). They are written as orders of magnitude, with the word "roughly", and never as one number:
   - Operating temperature: **roughly ten millikelvin** (a hundredth of a degree above absolute zero).
   - One-qubit gate time: **roughly ten to a hundred nanoseconds**.
   - Two-qubit gate time: **roughly tens to a few hundred nanoseconds**.
   - Readout time: **roughly a fraction of a microsecond to a few microseconds**.
   - Energy-loss time (T1) and phase-loss time (T2): **roughly tens to hundreds of microseconds**, with the best laboratory devices longer.
   - **Operations that fit in a lifetime:** computed from the ranges above (a lifetime divided by a gate time), shown as an order of magnitude ("tens to thousands"), and labelled "computed from the stated ranges; the ends of the ranges are not one device". This is the **only** computed comparison on the page.

   The page carries a **"figures change quickly" note** and a **dated "Sources and dates" list** in the same collapsible form as the Quantum Computing Today page, and prefers primary sources and review articles. **No vendor or product name, no qubit-count record, and no ranking** appear (the Today page names devices, and this module does not). The sources list is **not asserted in this draft**: it is built and checked when the ranges are. If the ranges cannot be sourced, the page states them as "orders of magnitude" only, and says so. FLAGS records that the facts are unreviewed.

4. **What does the main interaction show, and how does it relate to the taxonomy's wording?** The taxonomy says "Platform Card plus a chip-and-fridge explorer: select each part and see its role". *Recommendation:* a **Chip and Fridge** explorer built on a **schematic** (not a layout, not to scale, and labelled simplified) with six selectable parts, each a real button as well as a place in the picture:
   - **The qubit:** a small circuit with a junction in it.
   - **The coupler:** the element that links a qubit to its neighbour.
   - **The control line:** the wire that carries microwaves to a qubit.
   - **The readout resonator:** a small circuit beside the qubit whose response changes with the qubit's state, and its readout line.
   - **The cold stages:** the stack of colder and colder plates in the refrigerator, with the shielding.
   - **The wiring:** the many lines running from the room-temperature electronics down to the chip.

   Choosing a part shows, in separate places: **what it is** (a line), **what it does** (a line), **which of the five needs it serves** (start, control, connect, read, keep, with the number badge used on 04.01.01 and 04.01.05), **which source of noise it brings** (from 04.01.05's five: the environment, the control, the neighbours, leakage, the readout), and **the Platform Card question it answers** (a link to its place in the card). A **text list** of the six parts gives the same facts without the picture. The page labels it a model and not a device simulation. It is deliberately **not** a drawing of any real chip.

5. **How are "what is the qubit" and "why cold" taught?** *Recommendation:* the qubit is **an engineered artificial atom**: a circuit built from a **superconducting** metal on a chip, with a small element called a **Josephson junction** that makes its levels **unevenly spaced**, which is exactly the property 04.01.01 said a good qubit needs (so a drive can single out its two lowest levels). Reuse **`QedLadder`** to show the engineered ladder as a reminder, with the same invented units, labelled invented. The **transmon** is named once as the common design. Because it is made by fabrication, **no two qubits come out exactly alike** (04.01.01, and the "Why Qubits Differ" table of 04.01.05). **Why cold:** two reasons, said plainly. The metal has to be **superconducting**, which it only is when cold, and the qubit's two levels are **close in energy**, so even a little heat would jostle it, which is 04.01.01's "gap against heat" again. Show it with `QedLadder.startShares` at the three invented temperatures, as a **small comparison with the invented numbers of 04.01.01**, then state the real operating temperature as the approximate range of Question 3. Say once that **cold is not universal**: trapped ions and neutral atoms sit in vacuum and are handled with lasers, and the next pages show them. No circuit theory, no equations, no materials list.

6. **How are control and readout taught, and how are the earlier pages kept consistent?** *Recommendation:* **control:** a qubit is turned by a **microwave signal** sent down a wire (the "microwave signals" row of the 04.01.02 table, now with its platform). The signals are generated by electronics at room temperature, pass through the cold stages, and reach the chip. The shaped pulses, the electronics, and the scheduling are named as later depth (taxonomy decision 10) and not taught. **Readout:** a **resonator** sits next to the qubit, and **its response changes slightly depending on the qubit's state**, which is the "a circuit's response changes slightly" row of 04.01.03, now with its platform. A weak microwave probe is sent in, its changed reflection is **amplified** (named, not taught), and **04.01.03's two overlapping signals and the threshold are what come out**. The signal is weak, which is one reason a readout is slow (04.01.03). Say that the **real hardware's mechanism is one way of getting the signal from 04.01.03**, and that the invented axis on that page belonged to no platform. No dispersive-readout theory, no amplifier physics.

7. **How are speed and lifetime taught, and what is "fast but forgets quickly" said to mean?** *Recommendation:* the Platform Card's fourth question shows **three ranges** (a gate time, a readout time, and T1 and T2) and the **computed operations-that-fit** of Question 3, in the same wording as 04.01.04 ("the number of operations that fit is a budget, not a promise", and "a long time is not a guarantee"). The Quantum Computing Today page's promise is kept: this platform's gates are **fast** and its qubits **forget quickly**, said as a trade-off: **fast operations leave room for many of them inside a short memory**. The page says that **the comparison with the other platforms, and the claim that the two sides trade off, is 04.02.06's** and not made here. No ranking, no "better".

8. **How is "how do qubits connect" taught, and where does Module 05 begin?** *Recommendation:* **fixed on-chip neighbours.** Each qubit sits at a fixed place on the chip, linked to a few neighbours through a **coupler** (a fixed element, or one whose strength can be tuned, named and not derived). A two-qubit gate is performed **between neighbours that are linked**, which is the coupling idea of 04.01.02 with its platform. Layouts differ between devices, so the page shows **a generic pattern of nearest neighbours** and no vendor layout. Two qubits that are not linked need **extra operations** to bring them together, and what software does about that (routing, the coupling map, native gates) is **Module 05**, named once and not taught. Do not duplicate `QedBackend`'s field catalog.

9. **How is "what makes it hard to scale" taught, honestly?** *Recommendation:* **three reasons, each one line plus a small picture or number, with no record qubit counts.** (a) **Wiring and cooling:** every qubit needs its own control line and some readout wiring from room temperature down to the chip, and the refrigerator has limited room and limited cooling power. Show a **small invented wiring model** (qubits, lines per qubit, and one toggle for lines shared between qubits, in invented numbers, labelled invented): the number of lines grows with the number of qubits, and sharing helps but does not remove the growth. (b) **Fabrication:** no two qubits come out alike, so each must be measured and tuned (04.01.05's loop), and more qubits mean more tuning and more chance of a bad one. (c) **Crosstalk and a crowded chip:** more neighbours means more ways to disturb them (04.01.05's "neighbours"). Say that **how any of this is being worked on is not a Beginner topic**, and that the page does not predict a limit.

10. **How are the Bell strip and "what goes wrong here" shown, without ranking or inventing?** *Recommendation:* the module's recurring strip, **"how would this platform do it?"**, for the Bell circuit's three kinds of operation: a **one-qubit rotation** (a microwave signal on the qubit's control line), a **two-qubit interaction** (a gate between linked neighbours through the coupler), and a **readout** (the resonator). Reuse `QedCircuit`. Then **one short section on what usually goes wrong here**, tied to **04.01.05's five sources**, in words and without numbers: the **environment** (the cold stages and shielding are there to keep it out, and lifetimes are short), the **control** (microwave lines and electronics, and a setting that drifts), the **neighbours** (fixed neighbours disturb each other), **leakage** (levels outside the qubit's two), and the **readout** (a weak signal). Say plainly that **which source is largest depends on the device and changes over time**, as the Error Budget showed, and that this page ranks none. This section is **flagged for hardware review** (Question 3).

## Goal

Teach the learner that a superconducting qubit is a tiny engineered electrical circuit, kept cold, controlled and read with microwaves, fast and short-lived, linked to fixed neighbours, and hard to scale because every qubit needs wiring into a refrigerator, and that this is one answer to the same six questions every platform has to answer.

Central progression:

`THE FIVE NEEDS, AGAIN → A CIRCUIT THAT ACTS LIKE AN ATOM → KEPT COLD → CONTROLLED AND READ WITH MICROWAVES → FAST AND SHORT-LIVED → FIXED NEIGHBOURS → WIRING AS A LIMIT → THE BELL CIRCUIT'S THREE OPERATIONS HERE → TO BE CONTINUED`

Key conceptual transition: **"Hardware Fundamentals followed one invented qubit. A real platform answers the same questions with a real object, and every answer has a cost."**

It opens from the five needs and ends by handing the questions to a different physical system.

## Learning outcomes (Beginner)

The learner should be able to:

1. Say that a superconducting qubit is a tiny engineered electrical circuit that behaves like an atom with two usable levels.
2. Say that no two such qubits come out exactly alike, and that this is why each is measured and tuned.
3. Explain why the circuit is kept very cold, in two reasons, and say that cold is not universal.
4. Say that the qubit is controlled by microwave signals sent down wires from room-temperature electronics.
5. Say that it is read by a resonator whose response changes with the qubit's state, and that the signal is weak.
6. Say, in roughly stated terms, that its operations are fast and its qubits forget quickly, and read an operations-that-fit range as a budget and not a promise.
7. Say that its qubits sit at fixed places and interact with linked neighbours, and that unlinked qubits need extra operations.
8. Give three reasons it is hard to scale: wiring and cooling, fabrication variation, and a crowded chip.
9. Say how the Bell circuit's three operations are done on this platform.
10. Say which of 04.01.05's five sources of noise each part of the device brings, and that which one is largest depends on the device and the time.
11. Read the Platform Card's six questions, and say that the figures on it are approximate ranges that change quickly.
12. Ask how a different physical system would answer the same questions.

**Beginner (V1) teaches:** the circuit as an artificial atom, with the junction and the transmon named; why cold, in two reasons; microwave control and resonator readout in a sentence each; fast and short-lived, as roughly stated ranges and a computed budget; fixed neighbours and couplers; three reasons it is hard to scale, with an invented wiring model; the Bell strip; what usually goes wrong here, in words; the Platform Card; a figures-change note and a dated sources list; no real device, no vendor, no record, no ranking.

**Intermediate (future):** real device structure and parameter ranges; coupler designs; scaling architectures.

**Advanced (future):** current implementations and references.

Do not build Intermediate or Advanced content to make V1 more complete.

**Not on this page (any level):** how a gate turns a qubit, how a qubit is read, how a state fades, or what the sources of noise are (04.01.02 to 04.01.05, which this page uses and does not repeat); any other platform (04.02.02 to 04.02.05); the comparison between platforms, and any claim of better or worse (04.02.06); circuit quantization, the junction's physics, and the transmon's derivation; pulse shaping, control electronics, and scheduling; routing, coupling maps, native-gate sets, and how software uses them (Module 05); error vocabulary, codes, and decoding (Module 07); vendor names, product names, qubit-count records, and real device figures as single values; cross-module links beyond the few essential ones (deferred by the taxonomy).

## Pedagogical constraints (draft)

- Do not imply that a superconducting qubit is the "standard" qubit or the "best" platform. It is one answer to six questions, and its answers have costs.
- Do not imply that being cold is a property of quantum computing. It is a property of this platform.
- Do not imply that "fast but forgets quickly" is a verdict. It is a trade-off, and the comparison is 04.02.06's.
- Do not imply that a range is a device. The ends of a range are not one device, and every figure changes quickly.
- Do not imply that the schematic is a real chip, or that the wiring model's numbers are real.
- Do not imply that unlinked qubits cannot interact. They need extra operations, and how software handles that is Module 05.
- Do not imply that the sources of noise on this platform are ranked. Which is largest depends on the device and the time.
- Do not name a vendor, a product, a record qubit count, or a single device figure.
- Do not teach circuit quantization, pulse shaping, electronics, routing, coupling maps, error vocabulary, or any other platform here.
