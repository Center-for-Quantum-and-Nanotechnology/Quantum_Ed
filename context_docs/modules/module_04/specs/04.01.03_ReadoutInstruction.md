Goal
Teach the learner that reading a qubit is not looking at its state. A device detects a physical signal that is different for 0 and for 1. The signal is never exactly the same twice, so the two signals overlap, and a readout threshold that sorts signals into 0 and 1 will sometimes sort wrongly, not equally often for each, and slowly.
The central progression is:
A QUBIT YOU CAN TURN → A SIGNAL THAT DIFFERS FOR 0 AND 1 → A SIGNAL THAT IS NEVER THE SAME TWICE → OVERLAP → A THRESHOLD → MISREADS THAT DIFFER FOR 0 AND 1 → A SLOW STEP → TO BE CONTINUED
The learner should understand that Module 02’s measurement always told the truth because it was an idea, and that on a device it is a signal that has to be sorted.
The key conceptual transition is:
“Module 02 measured a qubit and always told the truth. On a device, a measurement is a signal that has to be sorted into 0 or 1, and some of it gets sorted wrongly.”
This section should prepare the learner for Coherence (04.01.04).
________________________________________
Learning Outcomes
By the end of the section, the learner should be able to:
	Say that a readout does not look at the state; it detects a signal that is different for 0 and for 1.
	Say that the signal differs on each platform, and name, for three kinds of qubit, what changes.
	Say that the signal is never exactly the same twice, so the signals for 0 and for 1 overlap.
	Describe a readout threshold as the value that sorts a signal into 0 or 1, and predict what moving it does to the two kinds of misread.
	Say that a misread of a 0 and a misread of a 1 need not be equally likely, and give two reasons: the threshold’s position, and a qubit that falls back during a slow read.
	Say that moving the threshold trades one misread for the other and cannot remove the ones whose signal already looks like the other state.
	Say that a device never sees which reads were wrong, and describe how it finds its readout errors anyway: prepare a known state, read it many times, and count.
	Say that a reported readout figure is a measurement of this readout, not a guarantee for a whole circuit.
	Say that reading ends the superposition (as in Module 02), and that the state left behind is the state found, which is not always the state reported.
	Say that a readout is slow compared with a gate, and give one reason.
	Distinguish a readout threshold from the error-correction threshold of Module 07.
	Ask how long a qubit stays what it was.

Page Structure
1. From Turning to Reading
Begin directly from the Gates and Control material.
Revisit the familiar progression:
A QUBIT THAT STARTS
↓
A SIGNAL THAT TURNS IT
↓
A STATE YOU CHOOSE, ALMOST EXACTLY
Then ask 04.01.02’s closing question:
“How do you find out what state it ended up in?”
Answer in one sentence: you do not look at it; you detect a signal that depends on it.
The learner should see this page as the physical side of Module 02’s measurement.

2. A Difference You Can Detect
Say that the state itself cannot be seen. A readout works by making the two states give different physical signals.
Show a small page-local picture: the |0⟩ level and the |1⟩ level, each with an arrow to “a lower signal” and “a higher signal”, both invented.
Name three kinds in a short table, with no mechanism, and the page that takes each up:
	Light is scattered or not — ions, and atoms by imaging. Taken up in: Trapped-Ion Qubits.
	A circuit’s response changes slightly — superconducting circuits. Taken up in: Superconducting Qubits.
	A detector clicks or does not — photons. Taken up in: Photonic Qubits.
Say that what changes is different on every platform, and that the axis used on this page is invented and belongs to no platform.
Use the page name, not the ID, for “Taken up in”.
Do not describe amplifiers, resonators, imaging, or how any signal is produced.

3. A Signal That Is Never the Same Twice
Show one static drawing of the two bell-shaped signals, each labelled, with the overlap between them marked.
Say in words that each read of the same state gives a slightly different signal, so each state gives a spread of signals, and that the two spreads can overlap.
Introduce noise in one plain sentence: the variation from read to read.
Name the three words once, as a preview that 04.01.05 turns into a distinction:
	The signal varies from shot to shot — noise.
	So some reads are wrong — a readout error.
	The share of wrong reads is the number a device reports — readout fidelity is the share that are right.
Do not give a formula, a standard deviation, or the kinds of noise that make the signal vary.
Ask:
“If a signal falls in the overlap, which state gave it?”
The learner should understand:
A SIGNAL IN THE OVERLAP
↓
COULD HAVE COME FROM EITHER STATE
↓
IT CANNOT BE SORTED WITH CERTAINTY

4. Main Interactive — “Read the Signal”
Make this the primary V1 interaction.
Allow the learner to set up a readout on an invented signal axis (0 to 12, invented units), move the threshold, and see where it goes wrong.
At minimum support:
	Prepared state: |0⟩, |1⟩, or an even split. This is the state the qubit is in when the read begins.
	How different the two signals are: Close, Medium, Far apart (the two bell-shaped signals centred 2, 4, or 6 units apart, each with the same invented spread of 1 unit).
	Slow-read fall-back: Off or On. When On, 10% of the shots that began in 1 give the 0 signal.
	Threshold: a slider from 0 to 12 in steps of 0.25, with a typed value, clamped. Default 4, deliberately off-centre so that the first look already shows a lopsided result.
	Read 100 shots.
	Find a threshold.
	Reset, which returns to Medium, fall-back Off, threshold 4, and prepared |0⟩.
The chance that a state’s signal lands on the wrong side of the threshold comes from a closed-form model of two bell-shaped signals. The even split’s chance of 1 comes from QedGates (an H on |0⟩), as in Module 02. Label the model simplified and every number invented.
When the learner changes a setting:
	Show the settings: the controls, and the threshold also drawn as a labelled line on the signal axis.
	Show the model’s exact answer: the two curves labelled “signal from 0” and “signal from 1”, the shaded tails that cross the threshold with the word “misread”, and the exact shares “a 0 is read as 1” and “a 1 is read as 0”, each as a bar with a percentage and a sentence. Reuse QedShots.renderBars. Tag it “computed from a simplified model”.
	Show the observation, only after the learner asks for it:
		After Read 100 shots, a strip of 100 dots, one per shot, placed at its signal. A circle means the shot really started in 0 and a square means it really started in 1. A misread carries a ✕. Then the true-by-reported table: two rows (really started in 0, really started in 1) and two columns (reported 0, reported 1). Head the rows “What this model knows” and the columns “What a device sees”. Say plainly that a device sees only the reported column. Tag it “simulated”.
		After Find a threshold, the measured total of misreads (out of 200) at each of 25 thresholds from 0 to 12 in steps of 0.5, drawn as open circles over the exact curve, the threshold with the fewest marked, the sentence that the bottom is flat so neighbouring thresholds look alike and a finer sweep would narrow it, and the measured shares at that threshold shown as a reported figure in the telemetry treatment (teal, dashed border, labelled “measured by preparing and reading”). Tag it “simulated”.
Define “true” in the table as the state when the read began. A shot that fell back counts as a misread of that state, because that is the question the device is asked. Say so on the page.
Say once that finding the threshold is the second of the measurements that calibration repeats, after the half turn in 04.01.02. The page does not recalibrate, does not move the learner’s slider, does not run over time, and does not say how often a device repeats it.
Keep what the learner changes (prepared state, signal difference, fall-back, threshold) separate from what is derived (the curves and the exact shares) and from what is observed (the dots, the counts, and the sweep).
The learner should visually experience:
THE SIGNALS, THE FALL-BACK, AND THE THRESHOLD
↓
THE EXACT SHARES MISREAD
↓
WHAT AN EXPERIMENT WOULD SHOW
Reinforce the distinction established in the taxonomy:
Readout is not looking at the state. It detects a signal, and the signals overlap.
Make the interaction resettable and keyboard accessible. Controls are native radio groups in option pills, a slider with a typed value, and real buttons. Give the curves a text description and put the data in a table inside a closed details element (the exact shares at the current settings, and the sweep’s 25 rows). Announce the new exact shares in an aria-live region when a setting changes, and the counts after a read. Keep focus where it was after the view re-renders. Give the dots strip a text summary of the counts. Present tables as real tables, with stacked rows on a phone.
Include these checks:
	Prediction task: “You lower the threshold while the qubit is in 0. Do more 0s get read as 1, or fewer?” The answer is more, because more of the 0 signal now lies above the threshold. Then compare with the explorer.
	Conceptual question: “Is a readout a look at the qubit’s state?” Give feedback on each wrong choice: it detects a signal that depends on the state, and the signals overlap, so a read can be wrong.
	Transfer task: “A device reads a qubit prepared in 1 and gets 0 about one time in ten, but reads a qubit prepared in 0 and gets 1 about one time in fifty. Is the threshold at fault?” The answer is maybe, but not only: the two misreads differ because the threshold is not centred or because some 1s fall back during a slow read. Moving the threshold trades one misread for the other, and the fall-back kind cannot be fixed that way. Then ask: “How could the device find out?” The answer is to prepare each state and count.
Do not add integration time, hardware detail, T1, a platform, kinds of noise, a confusion matrix, a correction of readout errors, mid-circuit readout, or real numbers.

5. Two Kinds of Misread
Explain what the explorer showed.
Moving the threshold trades one misread for the other. Lowering it reads more 0s as 1. Raising it reads more 1s as 0.
The two misreads can differ for two reasons. The threshold may not be in the middle. And a qubit that started in 1 can fall back during a slow read, so some 1s give the 0 signal. Say why in one sentence: a qubit can lose its energy while it is being read. Say that the cause is the next page. Do not name T1.
Moving the threshold cannot remove the second kind, because those shots already look like 0s.
Do not say that fall-back moves the best threshold. In this model the total of the two misreads is lowest at the middle threshold whatever the fall-back share.
Show the exact shares as a short table. For Medium signals with the threshold at the middle (5), each state is misread about 2.3% of the time with fall-back Off. With it On, a 0 is still misread about 2.3% and a 1 about 11.8%.
Say that a device does not want equal shares. It wants the fewest in total, and later pages return to what that costs.
Say that the fall-back switch is a simplified picture: a real fall-back is spread over the readout, not applied all at the start. It is not how every platform fails, and it is not the whole of readout error.
Do not imply that moving the threshold removes errors.

6. How a Device Finds Its Own Readout Errors
Say that a device never sees which reads were wrong.
It prepares a known state, reads it many times, and counts. Then it does the same for the other state. This is the same prepare-and-measure routine as the half turn on the previous page, now applied to the readout.
Point to the Find a threshold result as the worked case. The threshold is a setting the device finds, as the half-turn length was. Repeating that again and again is what 04.01.05 closes the loop on.
Show the best threshold’s measured misread shares in the teal reported-figure treatment, with its text label.
Add one sentence: a figure of this kind is a measurement of this readout, not a guarantee for a whole circuit.
Say that Module 05 covers how software is told such a number. Do not describe how.
Do not use confusion-matrix vocabulary, and do not correct for readout errors.

7. Reading Ends the Superposition
Use a short two-line before and after. Do not use an interaction.
	Before the read: an even split.
	After the read: whichever state was found.
Say that this is the idea from Measurement (02.07), and link to it.
Say, in one sentence, the real-device caveat: because the report can be wrong, the state left behind is the state found, which is not always the state reported.
Do not read a qubit twice, simulate collapse, or teach the Born rule.
Do not name non-destructive or mid-circuit readout, reset, or syndrome extraction.

8. Readout Is Slow
Reuse the 04.01.02 timeline (QedDrive.TIMELINE and timelineSVG) with the readout bar labelled. Do not draw a new picture.
Give one plain reason: the signal is weak, so it has to be collected for a while before a 0 and a 1 can be told apart.
Say that a longer look separates the signals better, but a slow readout is also time in which the qubit can fall back, and time the other qubits wait.
State the tendency only: a readout takes longer than a gate.
Show the Bell circuit’s three operations through QedCircuit as a drive, a coupled gate, and a readout, with the readout now explained.
Do not give durations, a number of gates per readout, or an integration-time control. Signal-to-noise against integration time is Intermediate.

9. From Reading to Keeping
End by comparing what Module 02 assumed with what this page showed.
Show a short table of five things, each against the Module 02 assumption, in the same order and wording as the earlier Module 04 recaps:
	The signal — a measurement was a look.
	The overlap — it told the truth.
	The misreads — they were the same for 0 and 1.
	The threshold — there was nothing to set.
	The time — it was instant.
Previously:
A MEASUREMENT
↓
A TRUE ANSWER
versus:
A READOUT
↓
A SIGNAL, A THRESHOLD, AND A CHANCE OF A WRONG ANSWER THAT DIFFERS FOR 0 AND 1
Present the closing question:
“You can start a qubit, turn it to a state you choose, and read it, with errors that differ for 0 and for 1. How long does it stay what it was?”
Then introduce the next problem:
A qubit does not keep its state for ever, and the way it fades has a name for each of two kinds of loss.
Do not explain T1, T2, or lifetimes here.
End with:
“How long does a qubit last?”

Important Pedagogical Constraints
Do not imply:
A READOUT = A LOOK AT THE STATE
Instead teach:
A readout detects a signal that depends on the state, and the signals for 0 and 1 overlap.
Do not imply:
A MISREAD OF A 0 = A MISREAD OF A 1
Instead teach:
They differ, because the threshold may not be in the middle and because a qubit can fall back during a slow read.
Do not imply:
MOVING THE THRESHOLD = REMOVING THE ERRORS
Instead teach:
It trades one misread for the other, and cannot remove the ones whose signal already looks like the other state.
Do not imply that a device sees its own mistakes. Only this model does.
Do not imply that the fall-back switch is how every platform fails, or that it is the whole of readout error.

The central takeaway should be:
Reading a qubit does not look at its state. A device detects a signal that is different for 0 and for 1, the two signals overlap, a threshold sorts them with errors that differ for 0 and for 1, and the whole step is slow.
