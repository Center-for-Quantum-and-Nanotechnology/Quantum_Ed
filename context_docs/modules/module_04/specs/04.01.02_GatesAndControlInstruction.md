This section is the second page of Hardware Fundamentals in Module 04. It follows Physical Qubits (04.01.01), whose qubit starts in |0⟩ and whose drive can single out its two levels, and it comes before Readout (04.01.03).
________________________________________
Goal
Teach the learner that a physical gate is a signal sent to a qubit, that the size of the turn it makes is set by the signal’s strength and length, and that this is why gates take time, are never exactly right, and need the qubits to be coupled when there are two.
The central progression is:
A QUBIT THAT STARTS → A SIGNAL SENT TO IT → STRENGTH × LENGTH = A TURN → NEVER EXACTLY RIGHT → TWO QUBITS NEED COUPLING → EVERY GATE TAKES TIME → TO BE CONTINUED
The learner should understand that Module 02’s turn on the sphere is the effect, and that on a device the signal is the cause.
The key conceptual transition is:
“Module 02 drew a gate as a turn on the sphere. On a device, a gate is a signal, and the turn is how strong it is and how long it lasts.”
This section should prepare the learner for Readout (04.01.03).
________________________________________
Learning Outcomes
By the end of the section, the learner should be able to:
	Say that a physical gate is a signal sent to a qubit, and name the kinds of signal in a sentence: microwaves, laser light, and magnetic fields.
	Say that the size of the turn is set by the signal’s strength and length, and predict what happens to the turn when either changes.
	Connect the signal to the picture in Module 02: the turn on the sphere is the effect, and the signal is the cause.
	Reach a half turn (|0⟩ to |1⟩) and a quarter turn (an even split) in the explorer, and read the chance of reading 1 from the turn.
	Describe the drive-and-flip experiment: repeat the drive at many lengths and watch the share that reads 1 swing up and down.
	Say that the turn a qubit makes is never exactly the turn that was set, and describe over- and under-rotation.
	Say that a miscalibration is a fixed offset, not randomness, and that it moves the length that gives a half turn.
	Say that two qubits can only affect each other if they are coupled, and describe, in one design, how that makes a gate conditional.
	Say that two-qubit gates are usually slower and less exact than one-qubit gates.
	Say that every gate takes time, that a circuit diagram shows order and not how long, and that the qubit’s state fades while gates run.
	Say what a native gate is, meaning what the device does directly, and that other gates are built from those.
	Ask how to find out what state the qubit ended up in.

Page Structure
1. From a Qubit That Starts to a Qubit That Moves
Begin directly from the Physical Qubits material.
Revisit the familiar progression:
A QUBIT THAT STARTS IN |0⟩
↓
A DRIVE THAT CAN SINGLE OUT ITS TWO LEVELS
Show the ladder from QedLadder with its drive arrow, as a reminder only.
Then ask 04.01.01’s closing question:
“How exactly do you move its state, and how far?”
Answer in one sentence: you send it a signal, and the turn depends on the signal.
The learner should see this page as the cause behind a picture Module 02 already drew.

2. A Gate Is a Signal
Introduce the control pulse: a signal of some strength that lasts some length, sent to a qubit.
Name the kinds of signal and who uses them in a short table of three, with no detail:
	Microwave signals — engineered circuits, and some spin qubits.
	Laser light — ions and atoms.
	Magnetic fields — spin qubits.
Say that the rectangle on this page is a simplified shape. Real signals are shaped, and that is later depth.
Say that a gate on a diagram is this signal in disguise.
Do not describe electronics, frequencies, or waveforms.

3. Strength × Length = Turn
Make the core relationship explicit.
Show the signal as a rectangle: its height is the strength, its width is the length, and its area is the turn.
Beside it show the sphere with the point turned by that angle.
Link to 02.04: a rotation about an axis is the effect, and this is the cause. The direction of the drive sets the axis (x or y).
Write the relationship as words and one small equation:
turn = rate × length
Explain it visually rather than relying on the equation.
Ask:
“If you make the signal twice as long, what happens to the turn?”
The learner should understand:
STRENGTH AND LENGTH
↓
THE AREA OF THE SIGNAL
↓
THE SIZE OF THE TURN
Do not imply that the rectangle is a real pulse.

4. Main Interactive — “Drive a Qubit”
Make this the primary V1 interaction.
Allow the learner to send a signal to one qubit that always starts in |0⟩, and see the turn it makes.
At minimum support:
	Direction: x or y (the drive’s direction sets the axis of the turn).
	Strength: Weak, Medium, Strong (15, 30, or 45 degrees of turn per unit of length, invented units).
	Length: 0 to 12 units, by a slider with a typed value, clamped, in steps of 0.5.
	Miscalibration: None; 10% weaker than set; 10% stronger than set.
	Try a half turn, which sets the length that would be a half turn if the strength were exactly as set (Weak 12, Medium 6, Strong 4).
	Measure 100 shots.
	Measure at every length.
	Reset, which returns to x, Medium, length 0, and no miscalibration.
The chance of reading 1 after a turn of θ from |0⟩ is sin²(θ/2). The sphere point comes from QedGates, as in 02.04, so the two pictures cannot disagree. Label the model simplified and every number invented.
When the learner changes a setting:
	Show the signal: the rectangle labelled “signal”, with the turn set in degrees. If a miscalibration is on, also show the turn made and the difference, with a text tag such as “off by 10%”.
	Show the effect: the sphere with the point after the turn, a text readout and a non-3D alternative, and the exact chance of reading 0 and 1 as bars. Reuse QedShots.renderBars.
	Show the observation, only after the learner asks for it:
		After Measure 100 shots, the counts of 0 and 1, seeded and tagged “simulated”.
		After Measure at every length, the exact curve of the chance of reading 1 against the length, with the measured shares as open circles over it (25 lengths from 0 to 12 in steps of 0.5, 100 seeded shots each), the length that gave the most 1s, and a sentence that the peak is flat, so several neighbouring lengths look alike and a finer sweep narrows it down.
Say once that this is the experiment a device repeats to find its half-turn length: the first of the measurements that calibration repeats. The page does not recalibrate, does not move any stored setting, does not run over time, and does not say how often a device repeats it.
Keep what the learner changes (direction, strength, length, miscalibration) separate from what is derived (the turn and the sphere) and from what is observed (the counts and the curve).
The learner should visually experience:
DIRECTION, STRENGTH, LENGTH, AND MISCALIBRATION
↓
THE TURN AND WHERE THE POINT ENDS UP
↓
WHAT AN EXPERIMENT WOULD SHOW
Reinforce the distinction established in 04.01.01:
A qubit that starts and a drive that can single out its levels are not yet a gate. A gate is a signal with a size.
Make the interaction resettable and keyboard accessible. Controls are real buttons, radio groups, and a slider with a typed value. Give the sphere a text readout and a non-3D alternative. Put the curve’s data in a table inside a closed details element. Announce the turn and the chance of reading 1 in an aria-live region when a setting changes. Keep focus where it was after the view re-renders. Present tables as real tables, with stacked rows on a phone.
Include these checks:
	Prediction task: “You keep the strength the same and double the length of the signal. What happens to the turn?” The answer is that it doubles. Then compare with the explorer.
	Conceptual question: “A circuit diagram shows an H, a CNOT, and a measurement in a row. Does it tell you how long each takes?” Give feedback on each wrong choice: it shows the order, and each takes a different time on a device.
	Transfer task: “You set a half turn, but the qubit gets 10% less strength than you set. Where does it end up?” The answer is a little short of |1⟩, so most runs read 1 and a few read 0. Then ask: “Is the offset random?” The answer is no: it is the same every time until the device is measured and set again.
Do not add detuning, a pulse shape, electronics, drift over time, recalibration, a readout mechanism, a lifetime, or a platform.

5. Never Exactly Right
Explain what the miscalibration showed.
The turn made differs from the turn set by a fixed share. The qubit ends just short of |1⟩ or just past it, and the chance of reading 1 is slightly under 100%: about 97.6% for a half turn that is 10% off.
The half-turn length found by the sweep moves under a miscalibration. With 10% less strength, the half turn takes longer; with 10% more, it takes less.
Say that an over- or under-rotation is an error of the gate, and that it is not random. It is a fixed offset until the device is measured and set again.
Say that a device has to measure its own half-turn length and measure it again, and that 04.01.05 asks where such offsets come from and why they drift.
Name tuning in one sentence: a signal that is not tuned to the step does less, which is why 04.01.01 mattered. Showing it correctly takes a tilted axis, which is later depth. Do not show a detuned drive on the sphere.
Do not imply that a miscalibration is random noise.

6. Two Qubits: Why Coupling
Use a short panel, not the main interaction.
Show a toggle, Coupling: Off or On, and a small table.
	Two qubits, A and B. A starts as |0⟩ or |1⟩ (two rows). B starts as |0⟩.
	A drive is tuned to the step B has when A is |1⟩. With coupling off, that is B’s only step.
With coupling off, B’s step does not depend on A, so the drive flips B in both rows. What happens to B cannot depend on A.
With coupling on, B’s step depends on A, so the drive flips B only when A is |1⟩. This is a conditional flip, which is what a CNOT is.
Say that this is one way to make a conditional gate, “in some designs”. Other platforms do it differently, through a shared motion or an excited state, and the platform pages show them.
Say that two-qubit gates are usually slower and less exact than one-qubit gates. State it as a tendency, with no figures.
Do not derive CNOT, name a mechanism, or show a circuit. 02.05 owns CNOT.

7. Gates Take Time
Use a short timeline block, not an interaction.
Show the Bell circuit’s three operations as bars of invented lengths, in the order of the circuit:
	A one-qubit drive — 1.
	A two-qubit gate — 4.
	A readout — 10.
Say that the diagram shows order, not how long, and that on every platform the lengths differ and the operations differ in length.
Say only the tendency: two-qubit gates usually take longer than one-qubit gates, and a readout usually takes longer than either. Readout is taught in 04.01.03.
Close with:
“The qubit’s state fades while this happens.”
Do not teach lifetimes.
Do not show real durations or a clock.

8. What the Device Does Directly
Use one paragraph and the Bell strip.
A device does a few operations directly: its own turns and its own two-qubit interaction. Every other gate is built from them. This is what makes a gate native, in the glossary’s sense. How software uses the list is the subject of Module 05.
Starting from |0⟩, a quarter turn about y gives the same state an H gives, which is why the Bell circuit’s first gate is a short drive. For other starting states H is a different move built from the device’s own turns, so do not say “H is a quarter turn”.
Show the Bell circuit’s three operations through QedCircuit as a drive, a coupled gate, and a readout. Say that readout is taken up next.
Do not list gate sets. Do not teach decomposition.

9. From Moving to Reading
End by comparing what Module 02 assumed with what this page showed.
Show a short table of the five things, each against the Module 02 assumption:
	The signal — a gate was a symbol.
	The turn — it did exactly what its name said.
	The offset — it was exact.
	The coupling — qubits interacted whenever the circuit said.
	The time — it was instant.
Previously:
A GATE
↓
A SYMBOL
versus:
A GATE
↓
A SIGNAL WITH A SIZE, A TIME, AND AN OFFSET
Present the closing question:
“You can start a qubit and turn it to a state you choose, almost exactly. How do you find out what state it ended up in?”
Then introduce the next problem:
Reading a qubit is also a physical act, and it is imperfect.
Do not explain readout here.
End with:
“How does a qubit become a bit?”

Important Pedagogical Constraints
Do not imply:
A GATE = AN INSTANT SYMBOL
Instead teach:
A gate is a signal that takes time.
Do not imply:
THE TURN YOU SET = THE TURN THE QUBIT MAKES
Instead teach:
A miscalibration is a fixed offset, and a real turn is never exact.
Do not imply:
A MISCALIBRATION = RANDOM NOISE
Instead teach:
It is the same every time until the device is measured and set again. Where offsets come from and why they drift is 04.01.05.
Do not imply that the rectangle is a real pulse. Real signals are shaped, and the rectangle is a simplified shape.
Do not imply that a one-qubit gate and a two-qubit gate are alike. A conditional gate needs the qubits to be coupled, and it is usually slower and less exact.
Do not imply that coupling off and on is how every platform makes a two-qubit gate. It is one design, and the platform pages show others.
Do not imply that an H is a quarter turn. From |0⟩ a quarter turn about y gives H’s result, and for other states H is built from the device’s own turns.
Do not imply that a circuit diagram is a timeline. It shows order, not how long.
Do not imply that the page’s numbers are real device values. They are invented and labelled.
Do not imply that finding the half-turn length is calibrating a device. It is one measurement that calibration repeats, and the loop is 04.01.05.
Do not imply that an on-target half turn always reads 1. The counts are samples.
Do not teach readout, lifetimes, noise kinds or drift, any one platform, pulse shaping, electronics, scheduling, detuning, native gate sets or decomposition, error correction, or real numbers here.
Keep Beginner V1 focused on the mental model required to understand why a qubit must then be read, and why that is also imperfect.
