This section is the fourth page of Hardware Fundamentals in Module 04. It follows Readout (04.01.03) and comes before Noise (04.01.05).
________________________________________
Goal
Teach the learner that a qubit’s state fades in two different ways, each with its own time. Each time is the scale of a gradual fall that is measured by preparing, waiting, and reading. The times set how many operations fit, and not whether a circuit works. And this is only part of what goes wrong.
The central progression is:
A QUBIT YOU CAN START, TURN, AND READ → A STATE THAT FADES → ENERGY LOSS (T1) → PHASE LOSS (T2) → MEASURED BY WAITING → A TIME SCALE, NOT A DEADLINE → HOW MANY OPERATIONS FIT → NOT A GUARANTEE → TO BE CONTINUED
The learner should understand that Module 02’s qubit kept its state for as long as you liked because it was an idea, and that a real qubit loses its energy and its timing at different speeds, and that a device has to measure each.
The key conceptual transition is:
“Module 02’s qubit kept its state for as long as you liked. A real qubit loses its energy and its timing, at different speeds, and a device has to measure each.”
This section should prepare the learner for Noise (04.01.05).
________________________________________
Learning Outcomes
By the end of the section, the learner should be able to:
	Say that a real qubit does not keep its state for as long as you like.
	Distinguish energy loss (a 1 falls toward 0) from phase loss (a superposition loses its timing), and say which time each one sets: T1 for energy loss, T2 for phase loss.
	Say that T1 and T2 are two separate numbers, and that one can be long while the other is short.
	Describe the wait-and-read experiment: prepare, wait, read, repeat, at many waits.
	Read a decay curve, and say what a wait of one T, and of three T, does to it.
	Say that T is the scale of a gradual fall and not the moment the qubit stops working.
	Explain why the phase experiment needs a second turn before the read, and why it ends at a coin flip and not at 0.
	Say that a time reported by a device is an estimate from a measurement, not a guarantee.
	Judge roughly how many operations fit within a time, using the lengths of a signal, a two-qubit gate, and a readout.
	Explain why a long coherence time alone does not guarantee that a circuit works, and name two other things that matter.
	Say that the fade comes from the qubit not being perfectly cut off from its surroundings, and that later pages say how.
	Ask where the rest of the imperfection comes from, and why it changes.

Page Structure
1. From Reading to Keeping
Begin directly from the Readout material.
Revisit the familiar progression:
A QUBIT THAT STARTS
↓
A SIGNAL THAT TURNS IT
↓
A READOUT THAT SORTS ITS SIGNAL
Then ask 04.01.03’s closing question:
“How long does a qubit last?”
Answer in one sentence: not for ever, and it fades in two ways.
The learner should see this page as the physical side of Module 02’s assumption that a qubit lasts as long as you like.

2. Two Ways to Fade
Introduce energy loss and phase loss side by side, each in two lines, with a small static picture of each: the flat side and top views at an early and a late wait.
	Energy loss — a qubit that was in 1 falls toward 0. T1 times it.
	Phase loss — a qubit in an even split keeps its odds, but loses its timing, so a later turn can no longer bring it back to a definite 1. T2 times it.
Say that T1 and T2 are two separate numbers, and that a device reports them separately.
Say that decoherence is the umbrella word for both losses, and define it once from the glossary.
Ask:
“Can a qubit keep its energy and still lose its phase?”
Answer yes, and say that Qubit B in the explorer shows it. How the two times are linked is later depth.
Do not use “lifetime” for both losses. Use “coherence time” for the general idea and T1 and T2 for the two numbers.

3. Measuring a Fade: Prepare, Wait, Read
Give the experiment in plain steps, for each loss.
	Energy: start in 1, wait, read.
	Phase: start in an even split, wait, turn it again, read.
Explain the second turn in one short passage. A plain read cannot see phase, and a qubit in an even split reads 0 or 1 at random whether or not its phase is intact. So a device turns the qubit again before reading. That turns an intact phase back into a definite 1, and a lost phase into a coin flip. This is the same “add a turn before the read” idea that Measurement (02.07) uses to read in another basis, and the turn is the quarter turn from Gates and Control (04.01.02).
Say that both experiments are repeated at many waits, and each wait is read many times.
Say that this is the wait-and-read experiment that calibration repeats, the second measurement after 04.01.02’s half turn and 04.01.03’s threshold, and that this page runs it once and changes nothing in response.
Add one line on T2: it is measured by this experiment, other measurements give other values, and a later page returns to that. Do not name T2*, the echo, or the relation to T1.
Do not show a formula.

4. Main Interactive — “Wait and Watch”
Make this the primary V1 interaction.
Allow the learner to pick a qubit and an experiment, choose a wait, and see how much of the state is left.
At minimum support:
	Qubit: A, B, or C, three invented qubits with their T1 and T2 in invented time units. The units are the same as the 04.01.02 timeline, where a one-qubit signal is 1, a two-qubit gate 4, and a readout 10.
		A, steady: T1 400, T2 300.
		B, phase fades first: T1 400, T2 50.
		C, energy fades first: T1 60, T2 80.
	Experiment: Energy (start in 1) or Phase (start in an even split).
	Wait: a slider from 0 to 600 in steps of 5, with a typed value, clamped. Default 0.
	Measure 100 shots, at the chosen wait.
	Measure at every wait, which runs the chosen experiment at 25 waits from 0 to 600 in steps of 25, 100 shots each.
	Reset, which returns to qubit A, Energy, and wait 0.
The learner picks a qubit and does not type T1 and T2. Each pair is chosen to be possible (every T2 is at most twice its T1), so the learner never sees an impossible qubit and the relation is never stated.
The chance of reading 1 after a wait of t is the exact value from the model:
	Energy: e to the power of minus t over T1. It starts at 100% and ends at 0.
	Phase: one half of one plus e to the power of minus t over T2. It starts at 100% and ends at 50%, a coin flip.
Do not show either formula. The starting state and the second turn come from QedGates, so the page agrees with Module 02 and 04.01.02. The phase model leaves out energy loss during the wait and says so. Label both models simplified and every number invented.
When the learner changes a setting:
	Show the settings: the controls, and a labelled “now” line on the plot.
	Show the model’s exact answer, tagged “computed from a simplified model”:
		The plot of the chance of reading 1 against the wait, always with both exact curves for the chosen qubit, energy solid and phase dashed, each labelled. T is marked on each curve by a labelled tick, computed as the wait at which about two-thirds of the fall has happened. An orange bracket from the starting level to the level now, labelled “lost so far”.
		The exact chance of reading 0 and 1 as bars with a sentence. Reuse QedShots.renderBars.
		The flat picture for the chosen experiment, with a text readout and a table (described under Visual / UX Requirements).
	Show the observation, only after the learner asks for it, tagged “simulated”:
		After Measure 100 shots, the counts of 0 and 1 at the wait.
		After Measure at every wait, the 25 measured shares as open circles over the curves, and the reported T for the chosen experiment in the telemetry treatment (teal, dashed border, labelled “measured by waiting and reading”). The page then says once: “The model’s own value was [T]. A device never knows it. Run it again and the estimate moves.”
Fit the reported T to the circles by a straight line through the logarithm of the part of the signal still left, over the circles that are still clearly above the noise. Report it to two significant figures. Say that the estimate is a measurement with uncertainty, not a guarantee. The sweep changes no setting, does not recalibrate, and does not say how often a device repeats it; 04.01.05 returns to that.
Keep what the learner changes (qubit, experiment, wait) separate from what is derived (the exact curves and the picture) and from what is observed (the counts, the circles, and the reported T).
The learner should visually experience:
QUBIT, EXPERIMENT, AND WAIT
↓
HOW MUCH OF THE STATE IS LEFT
↓
WHAT AN EXPERIMENT WOULD SHOW
Reinforce the distinction established in the taxonomy:
T1 is not T2. Losing energy is not the same as losing phase.
Make the interaction resettable and keyboard accessible. Controls are native radio groups in option pills, a slider with a typed value, and real buttons. Give the plot and the picture each a text description, and put the data in a table inside a closed details element (the exact chance at the chosen wait, and the sweep’s 25 rows). Announce the exact chance and the average in an aria-live region when a setting changes, and the counts after a read. Keep focus where it was after the view re-renders. Present tables as real tables, with stacked rows on a phone.
Include these checks:
	Prediction task: “A qubit starts in 1 and you wait for exactly one T1. About how many of 100 runs still read 1?” The answer is about 37. Then compare with the explorer.
	Conceptual question: “Qubit B has a long T1 and a short T2. Is its state mostly intact after a long wait?” Give feedback on each wrong choice: its energy is, and its phase is not, so a qubit in an even split has become a coin flip.
	Transfer task: “A device reports a long T1 for a qubit. Does a circuit on it work?” The answer is no: the gates, the readout, and everything else the next page names also matter, and the figure is an estimate at one moment. Then ask: “A circuit takes 15 time units. Qubit C has T1 60. Roughly what share of its excited runs fall in that time?” The answer is about a fifth.
Do not add sources of noise, drift, recalibration, T2*, an echo, a density matrix, a platform, real units, or error vocabulary.

5. A Time Scale, Not a Deadline
Explain what the explorer’s curves showed.
Each curve falls gradually. After one T, about two-thirds of the fall has happened: the share still excited has fallen to about 37% of where it started, and the phase experiment’s reading has moved about two-thirds of the way to 50%. After about three T, almost all of it has.
Say that T is a time scale, not the moment it ends, and that the fall starts at once.
Say that the energy curve ends at 0 and the phase curve at a coin flip (50%), and why: an energy loss ends in a definite state, and a phase loss leaves the odds as they were.
Say once that real decays are not always one clean exponential, and that one curve is a simplification used here.
Show the reported T from the sweep as an estimate.
Do not imply that a qubit works until T and then stops.

6. Where the Fade Comes From
Use one paragraph.
A qubit is never perfectly cut off, which Physical Qubits (04.01.01) said, so its surroundings can take away some of its energy and jostle its timing.
Name Module 07’s words once: Module 07 calls the first relaxation and the second dephasing, and it has names for the errors they cause, which this page does not teach. Link to the existing glossary entries.
Do not list sources, name materials, or give causes. Noise (04.01.05) owns them.
Do not use “channel”, Pauli, or any error model.

7. How Many Operations Fit
Use computed tables for the chosen qubit.
	How many operations fit in T1 and in T2: the time divided by the length of each of the three operations, from the 04.01.02 timeline (a one-qubit signal 1, a two-qubit gate 4, a readout 10).
	How long a qubit can wait before a share of its excited runs has fallen: the wait that costs 1%, 5%, and 10% of the energy. For qubit A these are about 4, 20, and 42.
	The Bell circuit: its three operations take 15 in all. Over that time a qubit like A loses about 4% of its excited runs, and one like C about 22%. Show the three operations through QedCircuit as a strip.
State the tendency only. Show no rule of thumb.
Ask:
“A qubit has a long T1. Does that mean a circuit on it will work?”
Do not give real times, units, or counts.

8. Not a Guarantee
Say, in four short points, that a long time is necessary and not enough:
	The gates themselves are slightly wrong (04.01.02).
	The readout misreads (04.01.03).
	The other imperfections come next (04.01.05).
	A time is a measurement at one moment, not a promise.
Say that Hardware Abstraction (05.04) makes the same point from the software side: a long T1 does not promise a good result.
Add one sentence: the three qubits differ by design, real qubits on one device differ from one another, and their times change, which the next page returns to. Make no claim about which platforms or how much.
Do not teach how software reports T1 and T2.

9. From Keeping to What Else Goes Wrong
End by comparing what Module 02 assumed with what this page showed.
Show a short table of five things, each against the Module 02 assumption, in the same order and wording as the earlier Module 04 recaps:
	The state — it was kept for as long as you liked.
	The energy — it was never lost.
	The phase — it was never lost.
	The time — it was not measured.
	The circuit — it could be any length.
Previously:
A QUBIT
↓
KEPT ITS STATE
versus:
A QUBIT
↓
LOSES ENERGY AND PHASE, AT DIFFERENT SPEEDS, MEASURED BY WAITING
Present the closing question:
“A qubit’s state fades with time, and T1 and T2 say how fast. But time is not the only thing that goes wrong. Where does the rest come from, and why does it change?”
Then introduce the next problem:
The gates are slightly wrong, the readout misreads, and the qubits disturb one another, and none of these stands still.
Do not explain the kinds of noise, drift, or recalibration here.
End with:
“Where does the rest of the imperfection come from?”

Important Pedagogical Constraints
Do not imply:
T1 = T2
Instead teach:
They are two separate numbers, and a qubit can have a long one and a short one.
Do not imply:
T = THE MOMENT THE QUBIT STOPS WORKING
Instead teach:
T is the scale of a gradual fall that starts at once.
Do not imply:
A LONG COHERENCE TIME = A WORKING CIRCUIT
Instead teach:
A long time is necessary and not enough. The gates, the readout, and the other imperfections matter too, and a time is an estimate at one moment.
Do not imply that a fall is always one clean exponential. It is a simplification here.
Do not imply that phase loss changes the odds of reading 0 or 1 in an even split. It changes whether a later turn can use it.
Do not imply that the three qubits, their times, or the operation counts are real device values. They are invented and labelled.
Do not imply that the reported T is exact. It comes from a fit to samples and changes from run to run.
Do not imply that T2 has one definition. It depends on the measurement.
Do not imply that the flat picture of the average is the whole of the state. It is a picture of many runs.
Do not imply that finding T is calibrating a device. It is one measurement that calibration repeats, and the loop is 04.01.05.
Do not teach the kinds of noise, drift, recalibration, T2*, echoes, the T1–T2 relation, density matrices, any platform’s figures, error vocabulary, or software’s description of T1 and T2 here.
Keep Beginner V1 focused on the mental model required to understand why time is not the only thing that goes wrong.

End State
The learner should leave with:
A QUBIT FADES
↓
ENERGY LOSS (T1) AND PHASE LOSS (T2), AT DIFFERENT SPEEDS
↓
EACH A TIME SCALE, MEASURED BY WAITING
↓
A BUDGET OF OPERATIONS, AND NOT A GUARANTEE
And understand the central relationship:
Wait → energy and phase fade at their own speeds → what a read would show → a time a device measures by preparing, waiting, and reading.
The central takeaway should be:
A qubit’s state fades in two ways, energy loss and phase loss, each with its own time. Each time is the scale of a gradual fall that a device measures by waiting, and it says how many operations fit, not whether a circuit works.
End with:
“Where does the rest of the imperfection come from?”
