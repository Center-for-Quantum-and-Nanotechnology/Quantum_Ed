Goal
Teach the learner that a trapped-ion qubit is one real atom held still by electric fields in a vacuum, with two of its own long-lived levels standing for 0 and 1, cooled, controlled, and read out with light, slow with a very long memory, linked to any other ion in its chain through shared motion, and harder to use as the chain grows, and that this is a second answer to the same six questions every platform has to answer.
The central progression is:
THE SIX QUESTIONS → A REAL ATOM HELD BY FIELDS → COOLED, CONTROLLED, AND READ WITH LIGHT → SLOW, WITH A LONG MEMORY → ANY PAIR IN A CHAIN → LONGER CHAINS ARE HARDER → THE BELL CIRCUIT’S THREE OPERATIONS HERE → TWO CARDS SIDE BY SIDE → TO BE CONTINUED
The learner should understand that the same six questions have a very different answer here, and that the cost is not wiring but speed and the length of the chain.
The key conceptual transition is:
“The same six questions have a very different answer here: the qubit is a real atom, held by fields and cooled by light, and its cost is not wiring but speed and the length of the chain.”
This section should prepare the learner for Neutral-Atom Qubits (04.02.03), by asking what would change if the atoms were uncharged and held by light in a grid.

Learning Outcomes
By the end of the section, the learner should be able to:
	Say that a trapped-ion qubit is one real atom with an electric charge, held still by electric fields in a vacuum, with two of its own levels standing for 0 and 1.
	Say that every ion of one kind is identical by nature, and that this does not make it error-free.
	Say how the ion’s motion is cooled, by light, and that the trap itself is often at room temperature, so that “cold is not universal” has a careful form.
	Say that the ion is controlled by light or microwaves tuned to its two levels.
	Say that it is read by whether it scatters light, bright or dark, and that the photon counts overlap so a misread is possible.
	Say, in roughly stated terms, that its operations are slow and its memory very long, and read an operations-that-fit range as a budget and not a promise.
	Say that any two ions in a chain can interact through shared motion, and that a longer chain makes this harder.
	Give three reasons it is hard to scale: longer chains, light to every ion, and the cost of moving ions between regions.
	Say how the Bell circuit’s three operations are done on this platform.
	Say which of 04.01.05’s five sources of noise the device brings, and that which one is largest depends on the device and the time.
	Read the Platform Card’s six questions for this platform, use the compare control to read two cards side by side without ranking them, and say that the figures are approximate ranges that change quickly.
	Find the sources behind the figures at the bottom of the page.
	Ask how a different physical system, with many uncharged atoms held by light, would answer the same questions.

1. The Six Questions, Again
Begin directly from the Superconducting Qubits material.
Revisit the familiar progression:
A SUPERCONDUCTING CIRCUIT, KEPT COLD
↓
A WIRE FOR EVERY QUBIT, FIXED NEIGHBOURS
↓
FAST, AND FORGETS QUICKLY
Then ask 04.02.01’s closing question:
“What if the qubits remembered much longer, and any two could interact?”
Show the empty Platform Card: the six questions, numbered, in the fixed order, and 04.02.01’s filled card shown small, as a reminder of the pattern.
Say that this page is the second answer, to the same six questions.
The learner should see a second platform as a second set of answers to questions already asked, rather than a new subject.

2. What Is the Qubit?
Introduce the qubit as one real atom with an electric charge, called an ion, so that electric fields can hold it.
It is held still in a vacuum, in a device called an ion trap. Name the trap, link it to the glossary, and do not derive it.
Say that two of the atom’s own internal levels, long-lived ones, stand for 0 and 1. Name the type of level at most once (hyperfine or optical), and name no ion species.
Say that every ion of one kind is identical, because nature and not fabrication sets its levels. Link back to 04.01.01 and to 04.02.01’s “no two alike”.
Say that identical does not mean error-free: the fields and the light that control it still vary.
Include a short explanation such as:
“Is it an engineered circuit?”
“No. It is a real atom, so its levels are set by nature. What is engineered is the trap that holds it and the light that controls it.”
This answers Platform Card question 1 in part.
Do not give equations, trap theory, or a species.

3. Held by Fields, Cooled by Light
Say that the ion’s motion is slowed with laser light, called laser cooling, until it is nearly still. Link to the glossary.
State the approximate range of its motion, roughly tens of microkelvin to a millikelvin, and that the trap is often at room temperature, and that some traps are cooled. Cite each.
Say the careful form of “cold is not universal”:
A SUPERCONDUCTING CIRCUIT IS COOLED AS A WHOLE BY A REFRIGERATOR
↓
AN ION’S MOTION IS COOLED BY LIGHT
↓
COLD LOOKS DIFFERENT ON EACH PLATFORM
Do not imply that being cold is a property of quantum computing. Do not teach how laser cooling works.

4. Main Interactive — “Ion Chain”
Make this the primary V1 interaction.
Allow the learner to build a chain, choose two ions, and step through the Bell circuit’s three operations on them.
At minimum support:
	Chain length: one of four invented counts, 3, 6, 9, and 12, chosen by a real radio group.
	The first ion and the second ion: two real selectors, and by selecting ions in the picture. Any two different ions are allowed, the nearest or the farthest.
	Rotate the first ion, Interact the pair, and Read out: three real buttons, in the order of the Bell circuit’s three operations. A later button is available only after the earlier ones, with a short note saying why.
	Reset, which returns to the first chain length, the first two ions, and no step done.
When the learner steps:
	Show the schematic (shown): the chain of ions between trap electrodes, inside a dashed vacuum boundary, with the light on the chosen ion (control blue, double outline), the shared-motion link between the chosen pair (gray, labelled “shared motion”), and the detector below (read-out brown, dash-dot, with an arrow from the ions to it), all labelled “simplified, not to scale”. It is a schematic and not a trap.
	Show the interaction (derived): the chosen pair, the invented time of the interaction for this chain length, labelled invented, and the words “any pair can interact”.
	Show the readout (observed, only after Read out): for every ion, bright or dark (a glyph and the word) and its photon count, tagged simulated, with the chosen pair agreeing and the other ions dark, and a line that the counts overlap, so a misread is rare but possible.
The learner should visually experience:
A PAIR OF IONS IN A CHAIN
↓
A LINK THROUGH SHARED MOTION, SLOWER IN A LONGER CHAIN
↓
BRIGHT OR DARK, FOR EVERY ION
Reinforce the distinction established in the taxonomy:
Every part of the picture answers a question the card asks, and brings a cost the card records.
Make the interaction resettable and keyboard accessible. Give a text table of the ions with the same facts as the picture. Give the picture a text description. Announce the result in an aria-live region after each step. Keep focus where it was after the view re-renders. Present tables as real tables, with stacked rows on a phone.
Include these checks:
	Prediction task: “You choose the first and the last ion in a long chain to interact. Can they interact, and what does a longer chain do to the interaction?” The answer is yes, any pair can, and a longer chain makes it slower and harder. Then compare with the explorer.
	Conceptual question: “An ion is read as dark. Does that mean the ion is not there?” Give feedback on each wrong choice: no, dark is one of the two results, 0, and an ion in 0 scatters little light, and the counts can overlap, so a misread is possible.
	Transfer task: “A platform has a very long memory and slow operations. Is it a good platform?” The answer is that this is a trade-off, that how many operations fit is a budget and not a promise, and that the comparison is later. Then ask: “Which of the five sources of noise would you look at first?” The answer is the five sources, and the device’s own measured figures.
Do not add a vendor, a real device, a species, a qubit count of a real machine, a ranking, or a derivation.

5. How Long, and How Fast
Show the card’s fourth row as ranges, each tagged “approximate” and cited:
	One-qubit gate: roughly microseconds to tens of microseconds.
	Two-qubit gate: roughly microseconds to hundreds of microseconds.
	Readout: roughly tens to hundreds of microseconds.
	Coherence time (T2): roughly seconds to minutes, with the best laboratory devices over ten minutes.
Then show the computed operations that fit in a lifetime, as an order of magnitude: tens of thousands to a million or more. Label it “computed from typical values in the sources; the ends of the ranges are not one device”. Say that it is a budget and not a promise, in the wording of 04.01.04 and 04.02.01.
Say in one line that the long times are phase-coherence times, T2, limited by stray fields, and not the fundamental lifetime of the state, and so the row is labelled “Coherence time (T2)” and not “T1 and T2”.
Say that the figures change quickly, and that the sources are at the bottom of the page.
Ask:
“A platform’s operations are slow, and its memory is very long. Is that good or bad?”
Answer: it is a trade-off. Slow operations leave a very long memory to fit many of them in. Whether that is an advantage depends on what else the platform can do. The side-by-side reading is the compare control’s, and the synthesis is the last page in this group.
Keep the Quantum Computing Today promise: remembers much longer, and runs more slowly.
Do not rank. Do not say “better” or “worse”. Do not give one number. Do not repeat any source’s own comparison between platforms.

6. How Qubits Connect
Show the ions in a chain, held together by the same fields, sharing their motion.
Say that they move together like a row of weights on a spring, so light that touches two of them can link their qubits through that shared motion. Name the analogy once and do not derive it.
The result is that any pair in the chain can interact directly. This is the coupling idea of 04.01.02 with its platform, and the opposite of 04.02.01’s fixed neighbours.
Show the cost, not only the benefit: a longer chain has more ways to move, so an interaction gets slower and harder to do well. Show it with the explorer’s invented rule, labelled invented.
Name Module 05’s fence once: that a device can describe every pair in its connectivity, and what software does with that, is Module 05. Link to 05.04 once. Do not duplicate QedBackend’s field catalog.
This answers Platform Card question 5.
Do not teach the motional modes. Do not draw any real device’s layout.

7. What Makes It Hard to Scale
Give three reasons, one line each, with a small picture or an invented number.
Longer chains are harder — more ions means more ways for the chain to move, so interactions slow down and get harder to do well (the explorer’s invented rule).
Light has to reach every ion — each ion needs light of the right kind aimed at it, and more ions need more of that.
Moving ions between regions is one way to scale — keep several short chains and move ions between zones to bring a pair together. State the cost: moving takes time and disturbs the ion’s motion, so it has to be cooled again [cite]. If practical, show it with a small invented toggle between two zones, labelled invented. Otherwise use one sentence and a static picture.
Say that how any of this is being worked on is not a Beginner topic, and that the page does not predict a limit.
This answers Platform Card question 6.
Do not state how many ions a chain can hold. Do not give a record qubit count.

8. The Bell Circuit, Here
Show the module’s recurring strip, “how would this platform do it?”, for the Bell circuit’s three kinds of operation. Reuse QedCircuit.
	A one-qubit rotation — a light or microwave pulse on the ion.
	A two-qubit interaction — light on two ions, through their shared motion.
	A readout — light scattered, bright or dark.
Then add one short section, what usually goes wrong here, tied to 04.01.05’s five sources, in words and without numbers:
	The environment — stray magnetic fields, and heating of the ion’s motion, which limit the memory.
	The control — the strength, aim, and timing of the light.
	The neighbours — light meant for one ion reaching the next, and ions disturbing each other.
	Leakage — the ion ends in a level outside its two.
	The readout — photon counts that overlap.
Read the five source names and their plain words at run time from QedNoise.SOURCES.
Say plainly that which source is largest depends on the device and changes over time, as the Error Budget showed, and that this page ranks none.
This section is flagged for hardware review.
Do not give a ranking or a number for any source.

9. The Platform Card, and Two Cards Side by Side
Show the complete card: six numbered rows, each with its question, its answer in plain words, and a tag where a value is approximate or not applicable. Each approximate value carries its numbered citation.
	Row 1 — What is the qubit? One real atom with an electric charge, held still by electric fields in a vacuum, with two of its own long-lived levels standing for 0 and 1. Its motion is cooled by laser light to roughly tens of microkelvin to a millikelvin. The trap is often at room temperature.
	Row 2 — How is it controlled? Light, or microwaves, tuned to the gap between the two levels.
	Row 3 — How is it read out? Light that only one state scatters: the ion shines (bright) or stays dark. A detector counts the photons.
	Row 4 — How long does it last, and how fast does it run? The ranges of section 5, and the computed operations that fit.
	Row 5 — How do qubits connect? A chain: ions held together share their motion, so any two can interact. A longer chain makes it harder.
	Row 6 — What makes it hard to scale? Longer chains, light to every ion, and the cost of moving ions between regions.
Show the note “Figures change quickly. They are approximate ranges taken from the sources at the bottom of the page, last checked 2026-11-07, and no range describes one device.”
Show the compare control: a real selector labelled “Compare with”, listing only the platforms whose cards exist, starting at “None”. Choosing one shows the two cards side by side, in the same six rows and the same order, this page’s platform first. Where two platforms word a row differently, show each platform’s own label and a one-line note that the two are not the same quantity.
Under the comparison, show its own Sources list, numbered in order of first citation within the comparison.
Say in one line: “A side-by-side reading is not a ranking. Figures change quickly, and the ends of a range are not one device.”
Say once that the next pages add the cards of the other platforms, and that the comparison explorer is the last page in this group.
Do not sort, highlight a larger or smaller figure, draw a chart or a bar, or use a word such as better, worse, or winner. Do not offer a comparison with a platform whose card has not been written, reviewed, and built.

Important Pedagogical Constraints
Do not imply:
A TRAPPED-ION QUBIT = THE BETTER PLATFORM, OR A SUPERCONDUCTING QUBIT = THE BETTER PLATFORM
Instead teach:
Each is one answer to six questions, and each answer has a cost.
Do not imply:
IDENTICAL IONS = ERROR-FREE IONS
Instead teach:
Nature sets the levels, but the fields, the light, and the readout still disturb the ion.
Do not imply:
ANY PAIR CAN INTERACT = INTERACTIONS ARE FREE
Instead teach:
Any pair can interact through shared motion, and a longer chain makes it slower and harder.
Do not imply that slow operations with a long memory are a verdict. It is a trade-off, and the comparison is 04.02.06’s.
Do not imply that a range is a device. The ends of a range are not one device, and every figure changes quickly.
Do not imply that the coherence times are lifetimes of the state. They are phase-coherence times, limited by technical dephasing.
Do not imply that being cold is a property of quantum computing. An ion’s motion is cooled by light, and the trap is often at room temperature.
Do not imply that the schematic is a real trap, or that the chain model’s numbers are real.
Do not imply that the sources of noise on this platform are ranked. Which is largest depends on the device and the time.
Do not imply that the compare control ranks, sorts, or picks a winner. It is a side-by-side reading of the same six questions.
Do not imply that a figure on the card is settled. It is a range taken from the sources, last checked on a date, and awaiting a hardware expert’s review.
Do not name a vendor, a product, a machine, an ion species, a record qubit count, or a single device figure, and do not state how many ions a chain can hold.
Do not teach trap theory, motional modes, gate schemes, pulse shaping, routing, coupling maps, error vocabulary, or any other platform here.
Keep Beginner V1 focused on the mental model required to understand why the answers depend on what the qubit is made of.

An atom → held by fields → cooled by light → controlled and read with light → slow, with a long memory → shared motion → a chain that gets harder as it grows.
The central takeaway should be:
A trapped-ion qubit is a real atom that answers the six questions with fields, light, and shared motion. Each answer has a cost, the figures are approximate and change quickly, and it is one answer among several, not a ranking.
